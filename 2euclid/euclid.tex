In this section we present an overview of Book I, which culminates in a proof of Pythagoras' Theorem (as seen on Byrne's cover). We begin by considering how Euclid's structure fits into the modern framework of an axiomatic system.


\begin{description}
	\item[\normalfont\emph{Undefined Terms}] E.g., point, line, etc. In fact Euclid attempted to define these: `A point is that which has no part,' and `A line has length but no breadth.'
	
	\item[\normalfont\emph{Axioms/Postulates}] In Euclid, an axiom is somewhat more general than a postulate.
	\begin{itemize}
	  \item[A1] If two objects equal a third, then the objects are equal\hfill ($=$ is transitive)
		\item[A2] If equals are added to equals, the results are equal\hfill ($a=c$ \& $b=d\implies a+b=c+d$)
		\item[A3] If equals are subtracted from equals, the results are equal
		\item[A4] Things that coincide are equal (in magnitude)
		\item[A5] The whole is greater than the part
	\end{itemize}
	Note the generality: the axioms seemingly refer to \emph{any} objects. By contrast, Euclid's postulates contain the \emph{geometry}: memorize these! 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item[P1] A pair of points may be joined to create a line
		\item[P2] A line may be extended
		\item[P3] Given a center and a radius, a circle may be drawn
		\item[P4] All right-angles are equal
		\item[P5] If a straight line crosses two others and the angles on one side sum to less than two right-angles then the two lines (when extended) meet on that side.
	\end{itemize}
\end{description}


The first three postulates describe \emph{ruler and compass constructions.} P4 allows Euclid to compare angles at different locations. P5 is usually called the \emph{parallel postulate.}
\medbreak

Euclid's system doesn't quite fit the modern standard, but the rough structure is there. We'll consider several serious shortcomings in later sections. For now we clarify two issues and introduce some notation.
\begin{description}
	\item[\normalfont\emph{Segments}] To Euclid, a line had \emph{finite} extent---we call such a \emph{(line) segment.} The segment joining points $A,B$ (postulate P1) is denoted $\cl{AB}$. In modern parlance a \emph{line} extends as far as permitted, often infinitely.
	\item[\normalfont\emph{Congruence}] Euclid uses \emph{equal} where modern mathematicians say \emph{congruent}. We'll express congruent segments and angles using modern notation: e.g., $\angle ABC\cong\angle DEF$. Equal angles/segments must be genuinely the same object (same location, etc.). otherwise said: equals implies congruence, but the converse is false.
\end{description}



\subsubsection*{Basic Theorems à la Euclid}

Theorems are typically presented as a \emph{problem}. Euclid provides a constructive solution (P1--P3), then proves that his construction really does solve the problem. Here is the first result of Book I.

\begin{thm}{I.\,1}{euclid1-1}
	Problem: to construct an equilateral triangle on a given segment.
\end{thm}

Note that a triangle is equilateral if its three sides are congruent.


\begin{proof}
	Given a line segment $\cl{AB}$:\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.68\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		By P3, construct circles centered at $A$ and $B$ with radius $\cl{AB}$.\smallbreak
		Label one of the intersection points $C$. By P1, we may construct $\cl{AC}$ and $\cl{BC}$.\smallbreak
		We claim that $\triangle ABC$ is equilateral.\medbreak
		Since $\cl{AB}$ and $\cl{AC}$ are radii of the circle centered at $A$, they are congruent. Similarly, $\cl{AB}$ and $\cl{BC}$ are congruent radii of the circle centered at $B$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.3\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={theorem 1 of book 1 - constructing an equilateral triangle on a given segment}]{euclid-I1}
	\end{minipage}\smallbreak
	 By Axiom A1, the three sides of $\triangle ABC$ are congruent.
\end{proof}

\pagebreak[3]

Euclid proceeds to develop several well-known constructions and properties of triangles.
\begin{itemize}%\itemsep0pt
  \item[I.\,4:] Side-angle-side (SAS) congruence: if two triangles have two pairs of congruent sides and the angles between these are congruent, then the remaining sides and angles are also congruent in pairs.
  \[
  	\begin{cases}
		  \cl{AB}\cong\cl{DE}\\
		  \angle ABC\cong\angle DEF\\
		  \cl{BC}\cong\cl{EF}
 	 	\end{cases}
  	\implies
  	\begin{cases}
		  \cl{AC}\cong\cl{DF}\\
		  \angle BCA\cong\angle EFD\\
		  \angle CAB\cong\angle FDE
  	\end{cases}
  \]
  \item[I.\,5:] An isosceles triangle has congruent base angles.
  \item[I.\,9:] To bisect an angle.
  \item[I.\,10:] To find the midpoint of a segment.
  \item[I.\,15:] (Vertical angles) If two lines/segments intersect, opposite angles are congruent.
\end{itemize}
Despite various logical issues in Euclid's presentation, his arguments are still worth reading (try Byrne's edition). We'll revisit these basic results in the Exercises and in the next two sections. 



\subsubsection*{Parallel Lines I: Construction \& Existence}\label{pg:parallelexist}

\begin{defn}{}{parallel}
	Lines are \emph{parallel} if they do not intersect. Segments are parallel if no extensions of them intersect.
\end{defn}

In Euclid, a line is not parallel to itself. The next result is one of the most important in Euclidean geometry, for it describes how to create a parallel line through a given point.


\begin{thm}{I.\,16.\ Exterior Angle Theorem}{extangle}
	If one side\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		of a triangle is extended, then the exterior angle is larger than either of the opposite interior angles.\smallbreak
		As pictured, $\delta>\alpha$ and $\delta>\beta$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.37\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={The exterior angle theorem}]{euclid-I16}
	\end{minipage}
\end{thm}

Euclid did not quantify angles numerically: $\delta>\alpha$ means $\alpha$ is congruent to some angle \emph{inside} $\delta$.

\begin{proof}
	Construct the bisector $\cl{BM}$ of $\cl{AC}$ \ (I.\,10).\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.66\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		Extend $\cl{BM}$ to $E$ such that $\cl{BM}\cong\cl{ME}$ (I.\,2).\smallbreak
		Connect $\cl{CE}$ (P1).\smallbreak
		The opposite angles at $M$ are congruent \ (I.\,15).\smallbreak
		SAS (I.\,4) applied to $\triangle AMB$ and $\triangle CME$ says $\angle BAM\cong\angle EMC$, which is smaller than the exterior angle at $C$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.33\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={proving the exterior angle theorem}]{euclid-I16p}
	\end{minipage}
	\smallbreak
	Bisect $\cl{BC}$ and repeat the argument to see that $\beta<\delta$: another invocation of the vertical angles theorem (I.\,15) is needed, this time at $C$.
\end{proof}


\pagebreak[3]


The proof in fact \emph{constructs} a parallel ($\cl{CE}$) to $\cl{AB}$ through $C$, as the next result shows.

\begin{thm}{I.\,27.\ Construction of a parallel}{}\phantom{bob}\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.66\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		If a line falls on two other lines such that the alternate angles ($\alpha,\beta$) are congruent, then the two lines are parallel.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.33\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={constructing a parallel using congruent angles}]{euclid-I27}
	\end{minipage}
\end{thm}

The \emph{alternate angles} in the exterior angle theorem are those at $A$ and $C$: $\cl{CE}$ really is parallel to $\cl{AB}$.

\begin{proof}
	Assume, for contradiction, that the lines are not parallel.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.66\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		WLOG suppose the lines meet on the right side at $C$.\smallbreak
		By the Exterior Angle Theorem (I.\,16), the angle $\beta$ at $B$, being exterior to $\triangle ABC$, must be greater than the angle $\alpha$ at $A$: contradiction.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.33\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={proof of the parallel line construction}]{euclid-I27p}
	\end{minipage}
\end{proof}

Euclid finishes the first half\footnote{Between I.\,16 and 27 are several results regarding comparisons of sides and angles of triangles (the triangle inequality), and further triangle congruence theorems such as ASA. We'll consider these later.} of Book I by combining this construction with the vertical angles theorem (I.\,15).

\begin{cor}{I.\,28.\ Alternative construction of a parallel}{euclid28}\phantom{bob}\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.66\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		If a line falling on two other lines makes congruent angles, then the two lines are parallel.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.33\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={constructing a parallel using congruent angles}]{euclid-I28}
	\end{minipage}
\end{cor}


Thus far Euclid has used only postulates P1--P4. In any model in which these hold:
\begin{quote}
	\texthigh{Given a line $\ell$ and a point $C$ not on $\ell$, \textbf{there exists} a parallel to $\ell$ through $C$}
\end{quote}




\subsubsection*{Parallel Lines II: Uniqueness \& Angle-sums}

Euclid finally invokes the parallel postulate (P5) to prove that the congruent alternate angle approach is the \emph{only} way to have parallel lines.

\begin{thm}{I.\,29.\ Uniqueness of parallels---converse to I.\,27}{euclid29}\phantom{bob}\par
	If a line falls on two parallel lines, then the alternate angles are congruent.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	We are given lines $\ell,m$ and a crossing line $n$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.66\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		Label the alternate angles $\alpha,\beta$ as in the picture, and let $\gamma$ be the supplementary angle to $\alpha$. We must prove that if $\ell$ and $m$ are parallel, then $\alpha\cong\beta$.\smallbreak
		Suppose to the contrary: WLOG we may assume $\alpha>\beta$.\smallbreak
		But then $\beta+\gamma<\alpha+\gamma$, which is a straight-edge.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.33\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={proving the uniqueness of parallels}]{euclid-I29}
	\end{minipage}\smallbreak
	By the parallel postulate, $\ell$ and $m$ meet on the left side of the picture: they are not parallel.
\end{proof}


\pagebreak[3]


The most well-known result about triangles is now within our grasp: the interior angles sum to a straight-edge. Euclid phrases this slightly differently.

\begin{thm}{I.\,32. Angle sum in a triangle equals a straight-edge}{}
	If one side of a triangle is extended, the exterior angle is congruent to the sum of the opposite interior angles.
\end{thm}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.64\linewidth}\vspace{-4pt}
	\emph{This is not a numerical sum,} though for familiarity's sake we'll often write \ang{180} for a straight-edge and \ang{90} for a right-angle.\smallbreak
	In the picture we've labelled the angles for clarity. The result amounts to showing that $\widetilde\alpha+\widetilde\beta\cong\alpha+\beta$.
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.34\linewidth}\vspace{-9pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={angle sum equals 180 degrees}]{euclid-I32}
\end{minipage}


\begin{proof}
	Construct $\cl{CE}$ as in I.\,16 so that $\widetilde\alpha\cong\alpha$. By I.\,17, $\cl{CE}$ is parallel to $\cl{AB}$.\smallbreak
	$\cl{BD}$ falls on the parallels $\cl{AB}$ and $\cl{CE}$, whence $\widetilde\beta\cong\beta$ (Corollary of I.\,29).\smallbreak
	Axiom A2 shows that $\angle ACD=\widetilde\alpha+\widetilde\beta\cong\alpha+\beta$.
\end{proof}

Since the three angles at $C$ sum to a straight-edge the angle sum in the triangle is plainly
\[
	\alpha+\beta+\gamma\cong \widetilde\alpha+\widetilde\beta+\gamma\cong\ang{180}
\]



\subsubsection*{Playfair's Postulate}

The parallel postulate is stated negatively (angles \emph{don't} sum to a straight-edge, therefore lines are \emph{not} parallel). Euclid likely chose this formulation to facilitate contradiction arguments, though it also obscures the meaning of the parallel postulate. Here is a more modern interpretation.

\begin{axiom}{Playfair's Postulate}{playfair}\phantom{bob}\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.64\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		Given a line $\ell$ and a point $C$ not on $\ell$, \textbf{at most one} parallel $m$ to $\ell$ passes through $C$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.34\linewidth}\vspace{-9pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={playfair's postulate}]{euclid-playfair-defn}
	\end{minipage}
\end{axiom}


Our discussion thus far shows that the parallel postulate implies Playfair.\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.62\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	\begin{itemize}
	  \item Let $A,B\in\ell$ and construct the triangle $\triangle ABC$.
	  \item The exterior angle theorem constructs $E$ and thus a parallel $m$ to $\ell$ by I.\,27.
	  \item I.\,29 invokes the parallel postulate to prove that this is the \emph{only} such parallel.
	\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.37\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={showing that the parallel postulate implies playfair}]{euclid-playfair3}
\end{minipage}\bigbreak

In fact the postulates are equivalent.

\begin{thm}{}{p5playfairequiv}
	In the presence of Euclid's first four postulates, Playfair's postulate and the parallel postulate (P5) are equivalent.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	We proved that P5 implies Playfair above.\medbreak
	For Playfair $\Rightarrow$ P5, we prove the contrapositive. Assume postulates P1--P4 are true and that P5 is \emph{false.} Using quantifiers, and with reference to the picture, we restate the parallel postulate:\footnotemark
	\begin{quote}
	  P5:\lstsp $\forall$ lines $\ell,m,n$, \ $\beta+\gamma<\ang{180}\implies\ell,m$ meet on the left side.
	\end{quote}
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.65\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		Its \emph{negation} (P5 false) is therefore:
		\begin{quote}
	  	$\exists$ lines $\ell,m,n$ for which $\beta+\gamma<\ang{180}$ and $\ell,m$ \textbf{do not meet} on the left side.
		\end{quote}
		That $\ell,m$ are parallel is Exercise \ref{exs:playfairlparallellemma}. By I.\,28, we may build a \emph{second} parallel line $\hat\ell$ to $\ell$ through the intersection $C$ of $m$ and $n$: as pictured,
		\[
			\hat\beta\cong\beta \implies \hat\beta+\gamma\cong\beta+\gamma<\ang{180}
		\]
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.33\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={the negation of the parallel postulate}]{euclid-playfair2}
		\smallbreak
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={constructing a second parallel through a point not on a line}]{euclid-playfair}
	\end{minipage}\smallbreak
	shows that $\hat\ell\neq m$. Crucially, these constructions only require postulates P1--P4.\par
	We therefore have a point $C$ not on a line $\ell$, though which pass (at least) \emph{two} parallels to $\ell$: this is the negation of Playfair's postulate.
\end{proof}

\footnotetext{%
	Throughout, $\ell$, $m$, $n$ are distinct lines, with $n$ intersecting both others. We strip this out for brevity.%
}



\subsubsection*{Non-Euclidean Geometry}\phantomsection\label{pg:sphere}

That Euclid waited so long before invoking the parallel postulate suggests he wanted to establish as much as possible in its absence. By contrast, everything from I.\,29 onwards relies on the parallel postulate, including the proof that the angle sum in a triangle is \ang{180}. For centuries, many mathematicians believed, though none could prove, that such a fundamental fact must be true independent of the parallel postulate.\smallbreak
Loosely speaking, a \emph{non-Euclidean geometry} is a model for which a parallel through an off-line point either doesn't exist or is non-unique. It wasn't until the 17--1800s and the development of \emph{hyperbolic geometry} (Chapter \ref{chap:hyper}) that a model was found which satisfies Euclid's first four postulates and the negation of the parallel postulate.\footnote{%
	The parallel postulate is therefore independent; in fact all Euclid's postulates are independent. They are also consistent (the `usual' points and lines in the plane being a model), but incomplete: a sample undecidable is in Exercise \ref{ex:euclidundecideable}.%
}\par

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.76\linewidth}\vspace{-5pt}
	We shall eventually see that every triangle in hyperbolic geometry has angle sum less than \ang{180}, though this will require a lot of work! For a more easily visualized non-Euclidean geometry consider the sphere. A rubber band stretched between three points on its surface describes a \emph{spherical triangle}: an example with angle sum \ang{270} is drawn. A similar game can be played on a saddle-shaped surface: as in hyperbolic geometry, `triangles' will have angle sum less than \ang{180}.
\end{minipage}

\subsubsection*{Pythagoras' Theorem}\phantomsection\label{pg:pythagoras}

Following his discussion of parallels, Euclid shows that parallelograms with the same base and height have equal area (I.\,33--41), before providing constructions of parallelograms and squares (I.\,42--46). Some of this is in Exercise \ref{ex:pythagexs}. Immediately afterwards comes the capstone of Book I.


\begin{thm}{I.\,47. Pythagoras' Theorem}{}
	The square on the hypotenuse of a right triangle equals (has the same area as) the sum of the squares on the other sides.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof} 
	The given triangle $\triangle ABC$ is assumed to have a right-angle at $A$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
		  \item Construct squares on each side of $\triangle ABC$ \ (I.\,46) and a parallel $\cl{AL}$ to $\cl{BD}$ \ (I.\,16).
		  \item $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{FB}$ and $\cl{BD}\cong\cl{BC}$ since sides of squares are congruent. Moreover $\angle ABD\cong\angle FBC$ since both contain \textcolor{Blue}{$\angle ABC$} and a \textcolor{mRed}{right-angle}.
		  \item Side-angle-side (I.\,4) says that $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle FBC$ are congruent (identical up to rotation by \ang{90}).
			\item I.\,41 compares areas of parallelograms and triangles with the same base and height:\vspace{0pt}
			\begin{align*}
				\operatorname{Area}(\mathcolor{mRed}{\square ABFG})&=2\operatorname{Area}(\triangle FBC)\\
				&=2\operatorname{Area}(\triangle ABD)\\
				&=\operatorname{Area}\left(\mathcolor{mRed}{\fbox{\phantom{'}}BOLD}\right)\\[-25pt]
			\end{align*}
			\item Similarly $\operatorname{Area}(\mathcolor{mGreen}{\square ACKH}) =\operatorname{Area}\left(\mathcolor{mGreen}{\fbox{\phantom{'}}OCEL}\right)$.
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{minipage} 
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.39\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={proving the pythagorean theorem}]{euclid-I47}
	\end{minipage}
	\begin{enumerate}[resume]
		\item Sum the rectangles to obtain $\square BCED$ and complete the proof.\hfill\qedhere
	\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}



Euclid finishes Book I with the converse which we state without proof. The argument is sneaky, and typical of Euclidean geometry---look it up!

\begin{cor}{I.\,48. Converse of Pythagoras'}{}
	If the squares on two sides of a triangle equal the square on the third, then the triangle has a right-angle opposite the third side.
\end{cor}

The other twelve books of the \emph{Elements} discuss further geometric constructions, including in three dimensions, alongside basic number theory including the Euclidean algorithm (Book VII).\medbreak

While undoubtedly a masterpiece of logical reasoning, Euclid's presentation has several flaws. Most problematic is his reliance on pictorial reasoning: for instance, he `proves' the SAS and SSS congruence theorems (I.\,4 \& 8) by laying one triangle on top of another, a process not justified by his axioms (see \href{http://math.furman.edu/~jpoole/euclidselements/euclid.htm}{here} or in \href{http://math.uci.edu/~ndonalds/Elements-I-VI.pdf}{Byrne}). In a modern sense, Euclid's approach is part axiomatic system and part model: his reasoning requires a visual/physical representation of lines, circles, etc. In part because of these issues, we now turn to a more modern description of Euclidean geometry courtesy of David Hilbert.

\subsection{Hilbert's Axioms I: Incidence and Order}\label{sec:hilbert1}

The long process of identifying and correcting the errors and omissions in Euclid's \emph{Elements} arguably culminated in David Hilbert's \emph{Grundlagen der Geometrie (Foundations of Geometry)} (1899). In the next two sections we consider some of the details of Hilbert's approach, thus providing a modern description of Euclidean geometry.\smallbreak

A version of Hilbert's axioms for plane geometry are listed on the next page.\footnote{%
	Like Euclid, Hilbert also covered 3D geometry, though we only give the axioms for plane geometry. With regard to Definition \ref{defn:consistency}, Hilbert's system is about as good as can be. Essentially one only one model exists (almost the same thing as completeness). The system is consistent in the absence of the continuity axiom though, in line with Gödel (Theorem \ref{thm:godel}), consistency cannot be proved once continuity is included. As stated, the axioms are not quite independent, though this can be remedied: O-3 does not require existence (follows from Pasch's axiom), C-1 does not require uniqueness (follows from uniqueness in C-4) and C-6 can be weakened slightly.%
}
The undefined terms consist of two types of object (\emph{points} and \emph{lines}), and three relations (\emph{between $*$, on $\in$} and \emph{congruence $\cong$}). For brevity we'll often (ab)use set notation, viewing a line as a set of points, though this is not necessary. At various places, definitions and notations are required.

\begin{defn}{}{hilbertbasic}
	Throughout, $A,B,C$ denote distinct points and $\ell,m$ lines.\vspace{-5pt}
	\begin{description}\itemsep 0pt %plus 1pt
	  \item[\normalfont\emph{Line}:] $\smash[t]{\lin{AB}}$ denotes the line through distinct $A,B$. This exists and is unique by axioms I-1 and I-2.
	  \item[\normalfont\emph{Segment}:] $\cl{AB}:=\{A,B\}\cup\{C:A*C*B\}$ has \emph{endpoints} $A,B$ and all \emph{interior} points $C$ between them.
	  \item[\normalfont\emph{Ray}:] $\ray{AB}:=\cl{AB}\cup\{C:A*B*C\}$ has \emph{vertex} $A$. In essence we extend $\cl{AB}$ beyond $B$.
	  \item[\normalfont\emph{Triangle}:] $\triangle ABC:=\cl{AB}\cup\cl{BC}\cup\cl{CA}$ where $A,B,C$ are non-collinear. Triangles are \emph{congruent} if their sides and angles are congruent in pairs.
		\item[\normalfont\emph{Sides of a line}:] $A,B$ (not on $\ell$) lie on the \emph{same side} of $\ell$ if $\cl{AB}\cap\ell=\emptyset$. Otherwise $A$ and $B$ lie on \emph{opposite sides} of $\ell$.
		\item[\normalfont\emph{Angle}:] $\angle BAC:=\ray{AB}\cup\ray{AC}$ has \emph{vertex} $A$ and \emph{sides} $\ray{AB}$ and $\ray{AC}$.
		\item[\normalfont\emph{Parallelism}:] $\ell$ and $m$ \emph{intersect} if some point lies on both: $\exists A\in\ell\cap m$. Lines are \emph{parallel} if they do not intersect. Segments/rays are parallel when the corresponding lines are parallel.
	\end{description}
	The pictures represent these notions in the usual model of Cartesian geometry.

\subsubsection*{\centering \large Hilbert's Axioms for Plane Geometry}

\columnsep 12pt
\begin{multicols}{2}

	\paragraph{Undefined terms}
	\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
	  \item \emph{Points}: \ use capital letters, $A,B,C\ldots$
	  \item \emph{Lines}: \ use lower case letters, $\ell,m,n,\ldots$
	  \item \emph{On}: \ $A\in\ell$ is read `$A$ lies on $\ell$' or '$\ell$ contains $A$'
	  \item \emph{Between}: \ $A*B*C$ is read `$B$ lies between $A$ and $C$'
	  \item \emph{Congruence}: \ $\cong$ is a binary relation on segments or angles
	\end{enumerate}
	
	
	\paragraph{Axioms of Incidence}
	
	\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
		\item[I-1] For any distinct $A,B$ \emph{there exists} a line $\ell$ on which lie $A,B$.
		\item[I-2] Distinct $A,B$ lie on \emph{at most} one common line.
	\end{itemize}\vspace{-2pt}
		
	Notation: \emph{line} $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ through/containing $A$ and $B$\vspace{-2pt}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item[I-3] On every line there exist at least two distinct points. There exist at least three non-collinear points.
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	\paragraph{Axioms of Order}
	
	\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
		\item[O-1] If $A*B*C$, then $A,B,C$ are distinct points on the same line, and $C*B*A$.
		\item[O-2] Given distinct $A,B$, there is at least one point $C$ such that $A*B*C$.
		\item[O-3] If $A,B,C$ are distinct points on the same line, exactly one lies between the others.
	\end{itemize}\vspace{-2pt}
		
	Definitions: \emph{segment} $\cl{AB}$ and \emph{triangle} $\triangle ABC$\vspace{-2pt}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item[O-4] (Pasch's Axiom) \ Suppose none of $A,B,C$ lie on $\ell$. If some point of $\ell$ lies on the segment $\cl{AB}$, then $\ell$ also contains a point of $\cl{AC}$ or $\cl{BC}$. 
	\end{itemize}\vspace{-2pt}
	
	Definitions: \emph{sides} of line $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ and \emph{ray} $\ray{AB}$


	\paragraph{Axioms of Congruence}
	\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
	  \item[C-1] (Segment transference) \ Let $A,B$ be distinct and $r$ a ray based at $A'$. Then there exists a unique point $B'\in r$ for which $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{A'B'}$. Moreover $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{BA}$.
	  \item[C-2] If $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{EF}$ and $\cl{CD}\cong\cl{EF}$, then $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{CD}$.
	  \item[C-3] If $A*B*C$, \ $A'*B'*C'$, \ $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{A'B'}$ and $\cl{BC}\cong\cl{B'C'}$, then $\cl{AC}\cong\cl{A'C'}$.
	\end{itemize}\vspace{-2pt}
		
	Definition: \emph{angle} $\angle ABC$\vspace{-2pt}
	
	\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
	  \item[C-4] (Angle transference) \ Given $\angle BAC$ and $\ray{A'B'}$, there exists a unique ray $\ray{A'C'}$ on a given side of $\overleftrightarrow{A'B'}$ for which $\angle BAC\cong\angle B'A'C'$.
	  \item[C-5] If $\angle ABC\cong\angle GHI$ and $\angle DEF\cong\angle GHI$, then $\angle ABC\cong\angle DEF$. Moreover, $\angle ABC\cong\angle CBA$.
	  \item[C-6] (Side-angle-side) \ Given triangles $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle A'B'C'$, if $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{A'B'}$, $\cl{AC}\cong\cl{A'C'}$, and $\angle BAC\cong\angle B'A'C'$, then the triangles are congruent.\footnote{%
	Its sides/angles are congruent in pairs. We extend congruence to other geometric objects similarly.%
}
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	\paragraph{Axiom of Continuity}
	
	Suppose a line/ray/segment $\ell$ is partitioned into non-empty subsets $\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2$ such that no point of one lies between two of the other. Then there exists a unique point $\mathcal O$ separating $\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2$:
	for all $A,B\in\ell$,
	\begin{quote}
		$A*\mathcal O*B$ if and only if $A,B$ lie in distinct subsets $\Sigma_1\setminus\mathcal O$, \ $\Sigma_2\setminus\mathcal O$
	\end{quote}
	
	
	\paragraph{Playfair's Axiom}
	
	Definition: \emph{parallel lines}\smallbreak
	
	Given a line $\ell$ and a point $P\notin\ell$, at most one line through $P$ is parallel to $\ell$.
\end{multicols}

	If distinct lines intersect, then they do so in exactly one point.

\subsubsection*{Axioms of Order: Sides of a Line, Pasch's Axiom \& the Crossbar Theorem}

\paragraph{Sidedness}

We now establish that a line has precisely two \emph{sides} (Definition \ref{defn:hilbertbasic}). This concept lies behind several of Euclid's arguments without being properly defined in the \emph{Elements.}




\begin{thm}{Plane Separation}{planesep}
	A line $\ell$ separates all points not on $\ell$ into two half-planes: the two \textbf{sides} of $\ell$. More explicitly, suppose $A,B,C$ are distinct points which do not lie on $\ell$. Then:
	\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
	  \item If $A,B$ lie on the \textbf{same} side of $\ell$ and $B,C$ lie on the \textbf{same} side, then $A,C$ lie on the \textbf{same} side.
	  \item If $A,B$ lie on \textbf{opposite} sides and $B,C$ lie on \textbf{opposite} sides, then $A,C$ lie on the \textbf{same} side.
	  \item If $A,B$ lie on \textbf{opposite} sides and $B,C$ lie on the \textbf{same} side, then $A,C$ lie on \textbf{opposite} sides.
	\end{enumerate}\vspace{-14pt}
	\begin{center}
		\tagpdfsetup{table/header-rows={2}}
		\begin{tabular}{c@{\qquad}c@{\qquad}c}
			\includegraphics[alt={plane separation case 1}]{hilbert-planesep}
			&
			\includegraphics[alt={plane separation case 2}]{hilbert-planesep2}
			&
			\includegraphics[alt={plane separation case 3}]{hilbert-planesep3}
			\\
			Case 1
			&
			Case 2
			&
			Case 3
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	We prove the contrapositive of case 1. Suppose $A,B,C$ are non-collinear. If $\cl{AC}$ intersects $\ell$, then $\ell$ intersects one side of $\triangle ABC$. By Pasch's axiom, it also intersects either $\cl{AB}$ or $\cl{BC}$.\smallbreak
	The other cases are exercises, and we omit the tedious collinear possibilities.
\end{proof}


Plane separation and sidedness allow us to properly define interiors of angles and triangles. 

\begin{defn}{}{interiorangle}
	A point $I$ is \emph{interior} to $\angle BAC$ if is lies on the same side of $\lin{AB}$ as $C$ and  same side of $\lin{AC}$ as $B$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{-5pt}
		Otherwise said, $I$ lies in the intersection of two half-planes.\smallbreak
		A point $I$ is \emph{interior} to $\triangle ABC$ if it is interior to all three angles $\angle ABC$, $\angle BAC$ and $\angle ACB$. Otherwise said, $I$ lies in the triple intersection of three half-planes defined by the triangle's sides.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={an interior point to an angle}]{hilbert-interior}
	\end{minipage}
\end{defn}

Interior points permit comparison of angles sharing a vertex: if $I$ is interior to $\angle BAC$, then $\angle BAI<\angle BAC$ has obvious meaning \emph{without resorting to numerical angle measure.}


\begin{cor}{}{angleinteriorexist}
	Every angle has an interior point. Every triangle has an interior point.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
	Given $\angle BAC$, consider any interior point $I$ of $\cl{BC}$. This plainly lies on the same side of $\lin{AB}$ as $C$ and on the same side of $\lin{AC}$ as $B$. Exercise \ref{exs:seginfinite} completes the argument for a triangle.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The Crossbar Theorem}

To paraphrase Pasch's axiom: \emph{if a line enters a triangle, it must come out.} We haven't quite established this fact, however. What if the line passes through a vertex?


 
\begin{thm}{}{crossbar}
	If $I$ is interior to $\angle BAC$, then $\ray{AI}$ intersects $\cl{BC}$ (the ``crossbar") at an interior point.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{-5pt}
		The result specializes to triangles. Suppose a line $\ell$ passes through a vertex $A$ and at least one interior point $I$ of $\triangle ABC$: then the line also contains an interior point of the side $\cl{BC}$ opposite the vertex.\vspace{2pt}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{-9pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={if a line enters a triangle through a vertex, it must cross the opposite edge}]{hilbert-pasch2}
	\end{minipage}
\end{thm}


\begin{proof}
	Extend $\cl{AB}$ to $D$ such that $B*A*D$ (O-2). Since $C$ is not on $\lin{BD}=\lin{AB}$, we have $\triangle BCD$. Since $\lin{AI}$ intersects one edge of $\triangle BCD$ at $A$ and does not cross any vertices (think about why\ldots), Pasch says $\lin{AI}$ intersects one another edge ($\cl{BC}$ or $\cl{CD}$) at some point $M$.
	\smallbreak
	The result follows from applying plane separation to the lines $\lin{AB}=\lin{BD}$ and $\lin{AC}$. First observe:
	\begin{quote}
		Since $I,M$ lie on the same side of $\smash{\lin{AB}=\lin{BD}}$ as $C$, it follows that $\smash{\cl{IM}}$ does not intersect $\smash{\lin{AB}}$. Since $A,I,M$ are collinear and $A\in\lin{AB}$, we moreover have \textcolor{mRed}{$A\notin\cl{IM}$}.
	\end{quote}
	\begin{center}
		\tagpdfsetup{table/header-rows={2}}
		\begin{tabular}{cc}
			\includegraphics[alt={the correct case of the crossbar theorem}]{hilbert-crossbar1}
			&
			\includegraphics[alt={the contradictory case of the crossbar theorem}]{hilbert-crossbar2}
			\\
			correct arrangement\footnotemark{}
			&
			contradictory arrangement
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	If $M\in\cl{BC}$, we are done. For contradiction, suppose $M\in\cl{CD}$. Relative to $\lin{AC}$:\vspace{-5pt}
	\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
		\item $I$ and $B$ lie on the same side since $I$ is interior to $\angle BAC$;
		\item $B$ and $D$ lie on opposite sides, since $B*A*D$ and $\lin{AC}\neq\lin{BD}=\cl{AB}$;
		\item $D$ and $M$ lie on the same side since $M\in\cl{CD}$ and $\lin{CD}\neq\lin{AC}$.		  
	\end{itemize}
	By plane separation, $I,M$ lie on opposite sides of $\lin{AC}$. The collinearity of $A,I,M$ then forces the contradiction \textcolor{mRed}{$A\in\cl{IM}$}.
\end{proof}

\footnotetext{%
	The pictures could be modified: e.g., $I=M$ and $A*I*M$ are also correct arrangements ($M\in\cl{BC}$).%
}

Euclid repeatedly uses the crossbar theorem without justification, including in his construction of perpendiculars and angle/segment bisectors (Theorems I.\,9 \& 10). We sketch the latter here.\smallbreak

	Quite apart from Euclid's arguments for SAS and SSS being suspect (we'll deal with these in the next section), he gives no argument for why $D$ is interior to $\angle BAC$ or why $\ray{AD}$ should intersect $\cl{BE}$!

\subsection{Hilbert's Axioms II: Congruence}\label{sec:hilbert2}

Hilbert's congruence axioms address two primary issues in Euclid.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Euclid uses of \emph{equal(s)} is confusing. In Hilbert, segments/angles are equal only when they are precisely the same (this amounts to the \emph{reflexivity} part of the next result).
  \item Euclid makes frequent unjustified uses of pictorial reasoning. For instance, his ``proof" of the SAS and SSS triangle congruence theorem (I.\,4) is little more that the claim that if one triangle is ``laid on top of the other," then they match up. It was eventually realized that one of the triangle congruences has to be an axiom: SAS is Hilbert's axiom C-6.
\end{enumerate}

We start with a small piece of bookkeeping.

\begin{lemm}{}{congequiv}
	Congruence of segments/angles is an equivalence relation.
\end{lemm}

\begin{proof}
  We prove the result for segments, leaving the statement for angles as an exercise.
	\begin{description}
  	\item[\normalfont\emph{Reflexivity}:] Let $\cl{AB}$ be given. Apply C-1 to obtain %\footnote{You could even take $A'=A$ and use the ray $r=\ray{AB}$!}
  	$\cl{A'B'}$ such that $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{A'B'}$. We sneakily use this \emph{twice} and apply C-2 to obtain
  	\[
  		\cl{AB}\cong\cl{A'B'}\text{ and } \cl{AB}\cong\cl{A'B'}\implies \cl{AB}\cong\cl{AB}
  	\]
  	\item[\normalfont\emph{Symmetry}:] Assume $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{CD}$. By reflexivity, $\cl{CD}\cong\cl{CD}$. By C-2 we have $\cl{CD}\cong\cl{AB}$.
  	\item[\normalfont\emph{Transitivity}:] Suppose $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{CD}$ and $\cl{CD}\cong\cl{EF}$. By symmetry, $\cl{EF}\cong\cl{CD}$. Axiom C-2 now shows that $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{EF}$. \qedhere
  \end{description}
\end{proof}


\subsubsection*{Segment/Angle Transfer and Comparison}

Hilbert's axioms of segment and angle transference are crucial for comparing non-collinear segments, and angles at different locations. These axioms help clean up Euclid's unjustified arguments based on pictorial reasoning.


\begin{defn}{}{lengthcomp}
	Let segments $\cl{AB}$ and $\cl{CD}$ be given.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		Axiom C-1 says there is a unique point $E$ on the ray $\ray{CD}$ such that $\cl{CE}\cong\cl{AB}$: we have \emph{transferred} $\cl{AB}$ onto $\ray{CD}$.\smallbreak
	We write $\cl{AB}<\cl{CD}$ if $E$ lies between $C$ and $D$ (pictured);\par
	similarly, if $C*D*E$, then $\cl{AB}>\cl{CD}$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.39\linewidth}\vspace{-18pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={transferring a segment onto a ray}]{angles-compare}
	\end{minipage}

\end{defn}

By O-3, any two segments are comparable: given $\cl{AB}$ \& $\cl{CD}$, precisely one of the following holds,
\[
	\cl{AB}<\cl{CD}, \qquad \cl{CD}<\cl{AB}, \qquad\cl{AB}\cong\cl{CD}
\]
Moreover, axiom C-3 says that congruence respects `addition' of adjacent congruent segments. Unique angle transfer, comparison and addition follow similarly from axiom C-4 and Definition \ref{defn:interiorangle} (interior points).\smallbreak

Neither Hilbert nor Euclid use or require an \emph{absolute} notion of length or angle-measure: the comparison $\cl{AB}<\cl{CD}$ does not indicate a relationship between numerical quantities (lengths). Introducing numerical length requires the inclusion of the real numbers (and thus far more axioms)---for purity reasons, we postpone this until later in this chapter.
 



\subsubsection*{The Triangle Congruence Theorems: SAS, ASA, SSS \& SAA}

Hilbert assumes side-angle-side (SAS) as an axiom (C-6) and proceeds to prove the remaining results. Here is ASA: we'll cover SSS momentarily, and SAA is Exercise \ref{exs:saaproof}.

\begin{thm}{Angle-Side-Angle, Euclid I.\,26, case I}{asa}
	Suppose $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle DEF$ satisfy
	\[
		\textcolor{dMagenta}{\angle ABC}\cong\textcolor{dMagenta}{\angle DEF},\qquad 
		\textcolor{Blue}{\cl{AB}}\cong\textcolor{Blue}{\cl{DE}},\qquad 
		\textcolor{dRed}{\angle BAC}\cong\textcolor{dRed}{\angle EDF}
	\]
	Then the triangles are congruent \ ($\angle ACB\cong\angle DFE$, \ $\cl{AC}\cong\cl{DF}$ and $\cl{BC}\cong\cl{EF}$).
\end{thm}


It is important to see that the triangle congruence theorems are independent of Playfair's Axiom (uniqueness of parallels). As such, we cannot simply assert $\angle ACB\cong\angle DFE$ and apply SAS.\smallbreak


Hilbert's approach modifies Euclid's: instead of laying $\triangle ABC$ on top of $\triangle DEF$, he creates a new triangle $\triangle DEG\cong \triangle ABC$ and proves that $G=F$.

\begin{proof}
	Segment transfer provides the unique point $G\in \ray{EF}$ such that $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\cl{EG}}\cong \mathcolor{mGreen}{\cl{BC}}$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.67\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		Apply SAS to $\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AB}}\cong\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{DE}}$, $\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle ABC}\cong\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle DEG}\, (=\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle DEF})$, $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\cl{BC}}\cong \mathcolor{mGreen}{\cl{EG}}$, to see that $\mathcolor{mRed}{\angle BAC}\cong \mathcolor{mRed}{\angle EDG}$.\smallbreak
		By assumption and the fact that congruence is an equivalence relation, $\mathcolor{mRed}{\angle EDG}\cong \mathcolor{mRed}{\angle BAC}\cong \mathcolor{mRed}{\angle EDF}$.\smallbreak
		Since $F$ and $G$ lie on the same side of $\lin{DE}$, angle transference (C-4) says they lie on the \emph{same ray} through $D$.\smallbreak
		But then $F$ and $G$ both lie on two distinct lines ($\lin{EF}=\lin{EG}$ and $\lin{DF}=\lin{DG}$). We conclude that $F=G$.\smallbreak
		By SAS we conclude the remaining data: $\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.31\linewidth}\vspace{-15pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={proving the A S A congruence by applying S A S}]{angles-asa}
	\end{minipage}
\end{proof}



\subsubsection*{Geometry Without Circles}

Circles are at the heart of Euclid's constructions (the \emph{compass} part of \emph{ruler-and-compass} constructions). Since circle intersections depend on continuity, Hilbert essentially ignores them. In the remainder of this section, we sketch a few of his alternative approaches to Euclid's basic results.

\begin{thm}{Euclid I.\,5}{euclidiso}
	An isosceles triangle has congruent base angles.
\end{thm}

Isosceles means \emph{equal legs}: two sides of the triangle are assumed congruent. The remaining side is the \emph{base.} Euclid relies on a famously complicated construction (look it up!). Hilbert does things more sneakily by relabelling the original triangle and applying SAS.

\begin{proof}
	Suppose $\triangle ABC$ is isosceles where $\textcolor{Blue}{\cl{AB}}\cong\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AC}}$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
 		Define a `new' triangle $\triangle A'B'C'=\triangle ACB$ by switching the base points:
		\[
			A':=A,\qquad B':=C,\qquad C':=B \tag{$\ast$}
		\]
		By axiom C-5, angles with reversed legs are congruent:
		\[
			\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle BAC}\cong\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle CAB}
		\]
		Relabelling the right side of this and both sides of our original assumption $\textcolor{Blue}{\cl{AB}}\cong\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AC}}$, we obtain
		\[
			\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AB}}\cong\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{A'B'}},\quad
			\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle BAC}\cong\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle B'A'C'},\quad
			\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AC}}\cong\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{A'C'}}
		\]
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{-10pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={proving that an isosceles triangle has congruent base angles by reflecting the triangle}]{angles-isos2}
	\end{minipage}\par
	We now have SAS! In conclusion, $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\angle ABC\cong\angle A'B'C'}$ is congruent to $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\angle ACB}$ by relabeling.
\end{proof}

Is Hilbert's argument really all that different from Thales' (page \pageref{pg:thales})? All he's done is to 'reflect' the original triangle, but in a manner consistent with the axioms!

\begin{thm}{Euclid I.\,12}{}
	To drop a perpendicular from a given point to a line.
\end{thm}

Euclid accomplishes this using circle intersections and SSS.\footnote{%
	Consider a picture similar to Thm.\,I.\,11 in Exercise \ref*{sec:hilbert1}.\ref{ex:pythagexs}.%

Hilbert sticks with segment/angle transference and sidedness before appealing to SAS (axiom C-6).
	
\begin{proof}
	Suppose $P$ dos not lie on $\ell$. Our goal is to construct $M\in\ell$ such that $\cl{PM}$ meets $\ell$ in a right-angle.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.69\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		Let $A,B$ be distinct points on $\ell$ (axiom I-3) so that $\ell=\lin{AB}$.\smallbreak
		By axioms C-4 and C-1, transfer $\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AP}}$ to the other side of $\ell$ at $A$, creating a new point $Q$.\smallbreak
		Since $P$ and $Q$ lie on opposite sides of $\ell$, the line intersects $\cl{PQ}$ at some point $M$. There are two cases to consider.
		\begin{itemize}
		  \item In the generic case $M\neq A$ (pictured), SAS applied to $\triangle MAP$ and $\triangle MAQ$ shows that $\angle AMP\cong\angle AMQ$. Since these angles sum to a straight-edge ($\cl{PQ}$), they are both right-angles.
		  \item	In the extreme case $M=A$, there are no triangles and SAS cannot be applied. Instead, observe that $B$ does not lie on $\cl{PQ}$ (which axioms/results make this clear?!) and apply the above argument with $B$ instead of $A$.
		\end{itemize}

\begin{thm}{Side-Side-Side, Euclid I.\,8}{}
	If $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle DEF$ have sides congruent in pairs,
	\[
		\mathcolor{dMagenta}{\cl{AB}}\cong\mathcolor{dMagenta}{\cl{DE}},\qquad 
		\mathcolor{Brown}{\cl{BC}}\cong\mathcolor{Brown}{\cl{EF}},\qquad 
		\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AC}}\cong\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{DF}}
	\]
	then the triangles are congruent ($\angle ABC\cong\angle DEF$, $\angle BCA\cong\angle EFD$, $\angle CAB \cong\angle FDE$).
\end{thm}

The strategy is similar to the proof of ASA: create a new triangle $\triangle DEG\cong\triangle ABC$\ldots


\begin{proof}
	Transfer $\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle BAC}$ to $D$ on the opposite side of $\lin{DE}$ to $F$, creating $G$ (axioms C-4 and C-1).\par
		SAS ($\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\cl{AB}}\cong\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\cl{DE}}$, $\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle BAC}\cong \mathcolor{Brown}{\angle EDG}$, $\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AC}}\cong\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{DG}}$) shows that $\triangle DEG\cong\triangle ABC$.\par

			\left.
			\begin{array}{l}
				\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{DF}}\cong\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{DG}}\\
				\mathcolor{mGreen}{\angle DFE\cong \angle DGE}\\
				\mathcolor{Brown}{\cl{EF}}\cong\mathcolor{Brown}{\cl{EG}}
			\end{array}
			\right\}
			\implies\triangle DEF\cong\triangle DEG
		\]

	\end{minipage}

Hilbert ignores Euclid's bisector approach.

\begin{proof}

By the crossbar theorem, we obtain the intersection point $E$. But now $\delta$ is an exterior angle to $\triangle EBC$ congruent to an opposite interior angle $\eta$ of the same triangle, contradicting part 1.
	\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Circles and Continuity}\label{sec:circ}

Circles were entirely natural to ancient cultures and could be created using a very simple machine (a peg and cord). As such, their intersections are central to Euclid's presentation. They are also very easy to define formally: all that's required is segment congruence and, if we want to define the inside and outside, segment comparison.

\begin{defn}{}{}
	Let $O$ and $R$ be distinct points. The \emph{circle} $\mathcal C$ with center $O$ and radius $\cl{OR}$ is the collection of points $A$ such that $\cl{OA}\cong\cl{OR}$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.74\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		Since all segments are comparable (Definition \ref{defn:lengthcomp}), every point satisfies exactly one of three possibilities:
		\begin{itemize}
		  \item $A$ lies \emph{on} $\mathcal C$ (that is, $\cl{OA}\cong\cl{OR}$).
		  \item $P$ lies \emph{inside} $\mathcal C$ if $P=O$ or $\cl{OP}<\cl{OR}$.
		  \item	$Q$ lies \emph{outside} $\mathcal C$ if $\cl{OR}<\cl{OQ}$.
		\end{itemize}
		Otherwise said: a circle partitions the plane into three subsets.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={a circle, exterior and interior points}]{circle-circle}
	\end{minipage}
\end{defn}

It seems obvious from the picture that if we want to travel from the inside of a circle to the outside, then at some point we must be \emph{on} the circle. Euclid relies on this ``obvious'' principle without justification. The modern \emph{Axiom of Continuity} provides exactly the necessary justification. This axiom is more technical than the others. As such, it is natural for Hilbert barely to mention circles; instead he builds as much geometry as he can using only the simpler axioms.\smallbreak

Two facts must be established in order to correct Euclid's approach.

\begin{thm}{}{}
	Let $\mathcal C$ and $\mathcal D$ be circles.
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item (Elementary/Line-Circle Continuity Principle)\lstsp If $P$ lies inside and $Q$ lie outside $\mathcal C$, then the segment $\cl{PQ}$ intersects $\mathcal C$ at exactly one point.
	  \item (Circular Continuity Principle)\lstsp Suppose $\mathcal D$ contains two points, one inside and the other outside $\mathcal C$. Then the circles intersect in precisely two points. The intersection points moreover lie on \emph{opposite sides} of the line through the circle centers.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}


\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}
	The idea of the first principle is to partition $\cl{PQ}$ into two pieces:
	\begin{quote}
	  \textcolor{Blue}{$\Sigma_1$} consists of the points lying on or inside $\mathcal C$.\smallbreak
	  \textcolor{mGreen}{$\Sigma_2$} consists of the points lying outside $\mathcal C$.
	\end{quote}
	One proves that $\Sigma_1$ and $\Sigma_2$ satisfy the hypotheses of the continuity axiom. The resulting point $\mathcal O$ is then shown to lie on $\mathcal C$ itself. Some of the details are in Exercise \ref{exs:elemcont}.\smallbreak
	The circular continuity principle is harder.
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	\hfill
	\includegraphics[alt={a segment intersecting a circle}]{circle-cont}
\end{minipage}


\pagebreak[3]



\begin{example}{}{}
	To convince ourselves why the continuity axiom is necessary, it is helpful to consider a model for which it is false. For ease of understanding, we use the language of co-ordinates.\smallbreak
	\emph{Rational geometry} $\Q^2=\big\{(x,y)\in\R^2:x,y\in\Q\big\}$ consists of those points in the Cartesian plane with rational co-ordinates. It satisfies almost all of Hilbert's axioms: C-1 and continuity are false.\par
	To see why, consider the picture, and the points with co-ordinates $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(1,1)$ and $\mathcal O=\big(\frac 1{\sqrt 2},\frac 1{\sqrt 2}\big)$. It should be clear that $\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AB}}\cong\cl{A\mathcal O}$ (both segments have length 1).\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{description}
			\item[\normalfont\emph{Axiom C-1}] Within $\Q^2$, we cannot transfer $\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{AB}}$ to the ray $\ray{AC}$, since $\mathcal O$, having irrational co-ordinates, is not a point in the geometry.
			\item[\normalfont\emph{Continuity}] Consider the circle centered at $A$ with radius 1. Plainly $A$ lies inside this circle and $C$ outside. However the segment $\cl{AC}$ does not intersect the circle, thus contradicting the elementary continuity principle.\par
			More properly, $\cl{AC}=\mathcolor{Blue}{\Sigma_1}\cup\mathcolor{mGreen}{\Sigma_2}$ may be partitioned as shown and yet no point $\mathcal O$ in the geometry separates $\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2$.
		\end{description}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={rational geometry fails the continuity axiom}]{circle-cont2}
	\end{minipage}
\end{example}




\subsubsection*{Equilateral triangles}

Armed with circle intersections, we can finally correct Euclid's very first proof!


\begin{thm}{Euclid I.1}{euclid-equil}
	An equilateral triangle many be constructed on a given segment $\cl{AB}$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	Following Euclid, consider the circles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ centered respectively at $A$ and $B$, both having radius $\cl{AB}$. We apply two of Hilbert's axioms and then the circular continuity principle.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{description}
			\item[\normalfont\emph{Axiom O-2}:] $\exists D$ such that $A*B*D$.
			\item[\normalfont\emph{Axiom C-1}:] Let $C\in\ray{BD}$ be the unique point for which $\cl{BC}\cong\cl{AB}$.
			\item[\normalfont\emph{Circular Continuity principle}:] $\beta$ contains $A$ (inside $\alpha$) and $C$ (outside $\alpha$). The circles therefore intersect in two points $P$ and $Q$.
		\end{description}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.38\linewidth}\vspace{-5pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={correcting euclid's first theorem}]{circle-euclid1}
	\end{minipage}
	\smallbreak
	The rest of the argument is essentially Euclid's. Since $P$ lies on both circles (and is therefore distinct from $A$ and $B$), we have $\cl{AP}\cong\cl{AB}$ and $\cl{BP}\cong\cl{AB}$: the three sides are congruent and so $\triangle ABC$ is equilateral.\footnotemark{}
\end{proof}

\footnotetext{%
	Strictly, this last step uses Lemma \ref{lemm:congequiv} and thus axioms C-1 and C-2, though it is not really possible to \emph{define} an equilateral triangle without it!%
}

As we saw in Exercise \ref*{sec:hilbert2}.\ref{ex:pythagnocont}, if Playfair's axiom regarding unique parallels is included, the above can be proved without circles or the continuity axiom. Regardless, we can finally say that every result in Book I of Euclid is correct, even if his original axioms and arguments are insufficient!



\subsubsection*{Basic Circle Geometry}

We continue our survey of Euclidean geometry with a few results about circles. Many of  these are found in Book III of the \emph{Elements}. For the rest of this chapter, we assume all of Hilbert's axioms including Playfair and Continuity; what follows often relies on their consequences, particularly angle-sums in triangles and the circular continuity principle.

\begin{defn}{}{circledef}
	With reference to the picture:\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{itemize}
		  \item A \emph{chord} $\cl{AB}$ is a segment joining two points on a circle.
		  \item A \emph{diameter} $\cl{BC}$ is a chord passing through the center $O$.
		  \item $\angle AOB$ is a \emph{central} angle and $\angle APB$ an \emph{inscribed} angle.
		  \item $\triangle ABP$ is \emph{inscribed} in its \emph{circumcircle.}
		  \item An \emph{arc} $\arc{AB}$ is part of the circular edge between chord points (\textcolor{dGreen}{major} or \textcolor{Blue}{minor} by length\footnotemark).
		\end{itemize}
	\end{minipage}

		\hfill

	\end{minipage}
\end{defn}

\footnotetext{%
	A minor arc corresponds to a (central) \emph{angle}: in pure Euclidean geometry, all angles are positive and less than or equal to a straight-edge.%

The following ideas should not be new, so most of the details are left as exercises.

\begin{thm}{III.\,20}{central-inscribed}
	If $\triangle ABP$ is inscribed in a circle such that $\arc{AB}$ is a minor arc, then the central angle is twice the inscribed angle: $\angle AOB=2\angle APB$.
\end{thm}

For a sketch proof, join $\cl{OP}$, breaking $\triangle ABP$ into three isosceles triangles before counting angle sums. A little extra is required if the center $O$ lies outside the triangle.


\begin{cor}{}{thalescor}
	\exstart (III.\,21)\lstsp If inscribed triangles share an arc, then the inscribed angles are congruent.
	\begin{enumerate}\setcounter{enumi}{1}
		\item (III.\,22)\lstsp An inscribed quadrilateral has opposite angles supplementary (summing to \ang{180}).
		\item (III.\,31---Thales' Theorem)\lstsp A triangle in a semi-circle is right-angled.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{cor}

Our final results consider the concept of \emph{tangency.} At first thought, you might suppose this to require some calculus, but it doesn't\ldots

\begin{defn}{}{}
	A line is \emph{tangent} to a circle if it intersects the circle exactly once.
\end{defn}

\begin{thm}{III.\,18, 19 (in part)}{tangentperp}
	A line is tangent to a circle if and only if it is perpendicular to the radius at an intersection point.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	We prove the ($\Leftarrow$) direction, leaving the other half as an exercise.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.62\linewidth}\vspace{-4pt}
		Suppose $T$ lies on a circle $\mathcal C$ and that $\ell$ passes through $T$ perpendicular to the radius $\cl{OT}$.\smallbreak
		Let $P$ be any another point on $\ell$. Since $\triangle OTP$ is right-angled, Exercise \ref*{sec:hilbert2}.\ref{ex:euclidi19} (angle-side comparisons) tells us that
		\[
			\angle OPT<\ang{90}\cong\angle OTP\implies \cl{OT}<\cl{OP}
		\]
		That is, $P$ lies \emph{outside} the circle.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.37\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={tangency means being perpendicular to the radius}]{circle-circle2}
	\end{minipage}\smallbreak
	Otherwise said, $T$ is the unique intersection point $\ell\cap\mathcal C$, whence $\ell$ is tangent to the circle.
\end{proof}


\begin{thm}{}{pointtangent}
	Any given point outside a circle lies on exactly two lines tangent to the circle.
\end{thm}

\subsection{Similar Triangles, Length and Trigonometry}\label{sec:similar}

In pure Euclidean geometry there are \emph{no numerical measures} of length or angle. Since segments and angles can be compared (Definition \ref{defn:lengthcomp}), we do have \emph{relative} measure. For convenience we've denoted right-angles and straight-edges by \ang{90} \& \ang{180} respectively. To avoid continued frustration, it is time we introduced explicit numerical measures, though to do so properly requires more axioms.

\begin{axioms}{Length and Angle (Degree) Measure}{euclidmeasure}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item[L1] To each segment $\cl{AB}$ corresponds a unique positive real number $\nm{AB}$, its \emph{length}.
		\item[L2] $\nm{AB}=\nm{CD}\Longleftrightarrow \cl{AB}\cong\cl{CD}$.
		\item[L3] $\nm{AB}<\nm{CD}\Longleftrightarrow\cl{AB}<\cl{CD}$.
		\item[L4] If $A*B*C$, then $\nm{AB}+\nm{BC}=\nm{AC}$.
		\item[A1] To each angle $\angle ABC$ corresponds a unique real number $m\angle ABC$ between 0 and 180, its  \emph{degree measure}.
		\item[A2] $m\angle ABC=m\angle DEF\Longleftrightarrow\angle ABC\cong\angle DEF$.
		\item[A3] $m\angle ABC<m\angle DEF\Longleftrightarrow\angle ABC<\angle DEF$.
	  \item[A4] If $P$ is interior to $\angle ABC$, then $m\angle ABP+m\angle PBC=m\angle ABC$.
	  \item[A5] Right-angles measure \ang{90}.
	\end{itemize}
\end{axioms}

In axioms L3 and A3, comparison of segments and angles has the meaning arising from the congruence axioms (Definition \ref{defn:lengthcomp}, etc.). It is not worth memorizing these axioms, just observe how they fit your intuition. Angle measure in Euclidean geometry has two notable differences from what you might expect:
\begin{itemize}
  \item (A1)\lstsp All angles measure strictly between \ang 0 and \ang{180}. In particular, a straight-edge isn't an angle (though such is denoted \ang{180}) and there are no \emph{reflex angles} ($>\ang{180}$). 
  \item (A2)\lstsp Angles are \emph{non-oriented,} measuring the same with reversed legs ($m\angle ABC=m\angle CBA$).
\end{itemize}
The axioms for length and angle follow the same pattern except that A5 explicitly fixes the scale of angle measure. To do the same for length requires a choice of some reference segment of length 1. The following is a consequence of the continuity axiom.

\begin{thm}{Uniqueness of measure}{}
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item Given $\cl{OP}$, there is a unique way to assign a length to every segment such that $\nm{OP}=1$.
	  \item There is a unique way to assign a degree measure to every angle.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.51\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	The segment $\cl{OP}$ in part 1 essentially provides the length-scale for a \emph{ruler.} We measure the length of any segment by moving this ruler on top of the desired segment (segment-transference).
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{-5pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={ruler axioms}]{similar-ruler}
\end{minipage}



\subsubsection*{Area Measure}

Including Playfair's axiom allows us to define \emph{rectangles} (Exercise \ref*{sec:euclid}.\ref{ex:pythagexs}).

\begin{defn}{}{}
	The \emph{area (measure)} of a rectangle is the product of its base and height (measures).
\end{defn}


\begin{minipage}[t]{0.83\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	Given a length measure, a square with side 1 necessarily has area 1.
	\smallbreak
	Relative to a base segment, the \emph{height} of a triangle is its \emph{altitude}: the length of the perpendicular dropped from the opposite vertex.
	\smallbreak
	Since every rectangle is a parallelogram, and every triangle half a parallelogram, Euclid's discussion (e.g., Thm.\,I.\,35) amounts to the familiar area formulæ:
	\[
		\operatorname{area}(\text{parallelogram})=bh,\qquad \operatorname{area}(\text{triangle})=\frac 12bh
	\]
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.15\linewidth}\vspace{6pt}
	\hfill
	\includegraphics[alt={area equals base times height}]{similar-areaheight}
\end{minipage}


While these expressions are nice to have, they are not necessary for what follows.


\begin{lemm}{}{triheightratio}
	If triangles have congruent bases, then their areas are in the same ratio as their heights. The same holds with the roles of heights and bases reversed.
\end{lemm} 

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.61\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	Length and area were non-numerical quantities to the ancient Greeks, so Euclid worked with length and area \emph{ratios}, an unnecessarily confusing approach for modern readers.\footnotemark{} To us, the Lemma is simply division of real numbers.
\end{minipage}

\subsubsection*{Similarity and the AAA Theorem}

Similar triangles are the concern of Book VI of the \emph{Elements.} All of this requires unique parallels.

\begin{defn}{}{}
	Triangles are \emph{similar,} written $\triangle ABC\sim\triangle XYZ$, if their sides are in the same length ratio\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{-10pt}
		\[
			\frac{\nm{AB}}{\nm{XY}}=\frac{\nm{BC}}{\nm{YZ}}=\frac{\nm{CA}}{\nm{ZX}}
		\]
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.3\linewidth}\vspace{-23pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={similar triangles have sides in a common ratio}]{similar-aaa}
	\end{minipage}
\end{defn}


Our primary result comes in two equivalent versions.

\begin{thm}{Angle-Angle-Angle/AAA, Euclid VI.\,2--5}{aaasim} \phantom{bob}\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.75\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{enumerate}
		  \item Triangles are similar if and only if their angles form congruent pairs.
		  \item Suppose a line $\ell$ intersects two sides $\cl{AB}$ and $\cl{AC}$ of a triangle at $D$ and $E$ respectively. Then
		  \[
		  	\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC\iff \ell\text{ is parallel to }\lin{BC}
		  \]\vspace{-15pt}
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.2\linewidth}\vspace{-23pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={A A A similarity theorem}]{similar-aaa1}
	\end{minipage}
\end{thm}

The above picture should convince you that $1\Rightarrow 2$ (uniqueness of parallels, Corollary \ref{cor:euclid28}, Theorem \ref{thm:euclid29}, etc.).\footnote{%
	This reliance on unique parallels is crucial: we do not expect AAA similarity in non-Euclidean geometry. Indeed we'll see in Chapter \ref{chap:hyper} that AAA is a \emph{congruence} theorem in hyperbolic geometry!%
} The converse of the equivalence $(2\Rightarrow 1)$ is left as an Exercise. We therefore restrict ourselves to proving part 2.

\begin{proof}
	$(\Leftarrow)$\lstsp Drop perpendiculars to create $h,k,d_1,d_2$ as pictured.\par
	\hspace*{\leftmargini}
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.57\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		Exercise \ref{exs:playfairparalleldistance} says $\ell$ is parallel to $\cl{BC}$ if and only if $d_1=d_2$.\smallbreak
		By Lemma \ref{lemm:triheightratio}, triangles with the same height have areas proportional to their bases:
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.36\linewidth}\vspace{-24pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={A A A similarity proof}]{similar-similarsides}
	\end{minipage}
	
	\vspace{-23pt}

	\begin{description}
		\item[]
		\begin{gather*}
			\frac{\nm{BD}}{\nm{AD}}
				=\frac{\operatorname{area}(\mathcolor{mGreen}{BDE})}{\operatorname{area}(\mathcolor{blue}{ADE})}
				\tag*{($\mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle BDE}$, $\mathcolor{blue}{\triangle ADE}$ share height $h$)}\\
			\frac{\nm{CE}}{\nm{AE}} 
				=\frac{\operatorname{area}(\mathcolor{mGreen}{CDE})}{\operatorname{area}(\mathcolor{blue}{ADE})} 
				\tag*{($\mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle CDE}$, $\mathcolor{blue}{\triangle ADE}$ share height $k$)}
		\end{gather*}
		Since $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle BDE}$ and $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle CDE}$ share base $\cl{DE}$, we see that
		\begin{align*}
			\ell\text{ is parallel to }\cl{BC}&\iff d_1=d_2 
				\iff \operatorname{area}(\mathcolor{mGreen}{BDE})
				=\operatorname{area}(\mathcolor{mGreen}{CDE})\\
			&\iff \frac{\nm{BD}}{\nm{AD}} =\frac{\nm{CE}}{\nm{AE}} \tag{$\ast$}
		\end{align*}
		Add $1=\smash{\frac{\nm{AD}}{\nm{AD}}=\frac{\nm{AE}}{\nm{AE}}}$ to both sides to obtain one part of the required similarity ratio
		\[
			\frac{\nm{AB}}{\nm{AD}} =\frac{\nm{AD}+\nm{BD}}{\nm{AD}} 
			=\frac{\nm{AE}+\nm{CE}}{\nm{AE}} =\frac{\nm{AC}}{\nm{AE}}
		\]
		It remains to see that this ratio equals $\smash{\frac{\nm{BC}}{\nm{DE}}}$. Again using common heights ($h,k$) of triangles,
		\begin{align*}
			&\frac{\nm{AB}}{\nm{BD}}
				=\frac{\operatorname{area}(ABE)}{\operatorname{area}(\mathcolor{mGreen}{BDE})} \quad\qquad
				\frac{\nm{CE}}{\nm{AE}} 
				=\frac{\operatorname{area}(BCE)}{\operatorname{area}(ABE)} \tag{$\dag$}\\
			\implies
			&\frac{\nm{AB}}{\nm{AD}} 
				\overset{\text{($\ast$)}}{=} 
				\frac{\nm{AB}}{\nm{BD}}\frac{\nm{CE}}{\nm{AE}} 
				\overset{\text{($\dag$)}}{=} 
				\frac{\operatorname{area}(BCE)}{\operatorname{area}(\mathcolor{mGreen}{BDE})}
			=\frac{\nm{BC}}{\nm{DE}}
		\end{align*}
		where the last equality follows since $\triangle BCE$ and $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle BDE}$ have common height $d_1=d_2$.

		\pagebreak[3]
		
		\item[$(\Rightarrow)$] Suppose $\triangle ABC\sim\triangle ADE$.\par
		\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
			Let $m$ be the unique parallel to $\cl{BC}$ through $D$. This intersects $\cl{AC}$ at a point $G$. We must prove that $G=E$ (consequently $m=\ell$).\smallbreak
			By the ($\Leftarrow$) direction above,
			\[
				\triangle ABC\sim\triangle ADG
			\]
		\end{minipage}
		\hfill
		\begin{minipage}[t]{0.37\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
			\hfill
			\includegraphics[alt={A A A similarity proof}]{similar-similarsides2}
		\end{minipage}\par
		However $\sim$ is transitive (it is an equivalence relation), whence $\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ADG$. The similarity ratio is 1 ($\nm{AD}:\nm{AD}$), whence
		\[
			\frac{\nm{AE}}{\nm{AG}}=1
			\implies \nm{AE}=\nm{AG} 
			\implies E=G \tag*{\qedhere}
		\]
	\end{description}\vspace{-30pt}
\end{proof}



\subsubsection*{Applications of Similarity}

We finish with several advanced applications of similarity that were not known to Euclid.\vspace{-5pt}

\paragraph{Trigonometry}

Early trigonometry dates to a few centuries after Euclid, though the approach was different.\footnote{%
	Ancient forerunners of sine and cosine were defined using chords of circles rather than triangles. The term \emph{trigonometry} (literally \emph{triangle measure}) wasn't coined until 1595.%
}

\begin{defn}{}{}
	Given an acute angle $\angle ABC$ ($m\angle ABC<\ang{90}$), drop a perpendicular from $A$ to $\ray{BC}$ at $D$ so that $\angle ADB$ is a right-angle. Define
	\[
		\sin\angle ABC:=\frac{\nm{AD}}{\nm{AB}}\qquad 
		\cos\angle ABC:=\frac{\nm{BD}}{\nm{AB}}
	\]
\end{defn}




\begin{thm}{}{sinewd}
	Angles have the same sine (cosine) if and only if they are congruent.
\end{thm}


\begin{proof}
	Assume $\angle ABC\cong\angle A'B'C'$, as pictured, and drop perpendiculars to $D,D'$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.56\linewidth}\vspace{-7pt}
		Since $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle A'B'D'$ have two pairs of mutually congruent angles, the third pair is congruent also. AAA therefore applies: the triangles are similar and
		\[
			\frac{\nm{AD}}{\nm{AB}}=\frac{\nm{A'D'}}{\nm{A'B'}}\qquad 
			\frac{\nm{BD}}{\nm{AB}}=\frac{\nm{B'D'}}{\nm{A'B'}}
		\]
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.43\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={well definition of trigonometric functions using similar triangles}]{similar-trig}
	\end{minipage}
	\smallbreak
	In particular, $\sin\angle ABC=\sin\angle A'B'C'$ and $\cos\angle ABC=\cos\angle A'B'C'$.\smallbreak
	The converse is an exercise.
\end{proof}


\pagebreak[3]


\paragraph{Cevians}

After Giovanni Ceva (1647--1734), a \emph{cevian} is any segment joining a vertex to the opposite side of a triangle. Here is a result from the height of Euclidean geometry---good luck trying to prove it using co-ordinates!

\begin{thm}{Ceva's Theorem}{}
	Given $\triangle ABC$ and cevians $\cl{AX}$, $\cl{BY}$, $\cl{CZ}$,
	\[
		\frac{\nm{BX}}{\nm{XC}}\frac{\nm{CY}}{\nm{YA}}\frac{\nm{AZ}}{\nm{ZB}}=1\iff\text{the cevians meet at a common point}
	\] 
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	Suppose the cevians meet at $P$, and apply Lemma \ref{lemm:triheightratio} twice.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.65\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
		\[
			\frac{\operatorname{area}(ABX)}{\operatorname{area}(AXC)}
			=\frac{\nm{BX}}{\nm{XC}} 
			=\frac{\operatorname{area}(PBX)}{\operatorname{area}(PXC)}
		\]
		A little algebra (exercise) shows that
		\begin{align*}
			\frac{\nm{BX}}{\nm{XC}} 
			&=
			\frac{\operatorname{area}(ABX)-\operatorname{area}(PBX)}{\operatorname{area}(AXC)-\operatorname{area}(PXC)}\\
			&=\frac{\operatorname{area}(ABP)}{\operatorname{area}(APC)}
		\end{align*}
		Repeat for the other ratios and multiply to get 1.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.34\linewidth}\vspace{0pt} 
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={ceva's theorem}]{similar-cevians}
	\end{minipage}\medbreak
	The converse is also an exercise.
\end{proof}


\paragraph{The Butterfly Theorem}

We finish with a beautiful result from roughly 1803--5. Several proofs are known; ours relies on similar triangles and a simple lemma whose proof is left as an exercise.

\begin{lemm}{}{butterflylemm}
	Let $\cl{AD}$ and $\cl{PQ}$ be chords of a circle which intersect at $X$. Then
	\[
		\nm{AX}\nm{XD}=\nm{PX}\nm{XQ}
	\]
\end{lemm}


\begin{thm}{Butterfly Theorem}{}
	Given a circle, suppose:\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.65\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{itemize}
		  \item $\cl{PQ}$ is a chord with midpoint $M$.
		  \item $\cl{AC}$ and $\cl{BD}$ are chords meeting at $M$.
		  \item $X=\cl{AD}\cap\cl{PQ}$ and $Y=\cl{BC}\cap\cl{PQ}$.
		\end{itemize}
		Then $M$ is the midpoint of $\cl{XY}$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.34\linewidth}\vspace{-22pt} 
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={the butterfly theorem}]{similar-butterfly}
	\end{minipage}
\end{thm}


\clearpage

\begin{proof}
	For convenience we introduce several numerical lengths:\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.57\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $z=\nm{PM}=\nm{MQ}$.
			\item $x=\nm{XM}$ and $y=\nm{YM}$.
			\item Drop perpendiculars from $X,Y$ to chords $\cl{AD},\cl{BC}$, to obtain $x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2$ as shown.
		\end{itemize}
		Our goal is to prove that $x=y$.\medbreak
		First observe that we have two pairs of congruent (vertical) angles at $M$, and two pairs of congruent inscribed angles at $\textcolor{mMagenta}{A,B}$ and $\textcolor{Brown}{C,D}$.\smallbreak
		By the AAA theorem, we now have several pairs of similar triangles, whose sides may be compared:
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.42\linewidth}\vspace{-15pt}
		\hfill
		\includegraphics[alt={proving the butterfly theorem using similar triangles}]{similar-butterfly2}
	\end{minipage}
	\par
	\begin{enumeratea}
		\item $\dfrac{x}{x_1}=\dfrac{y}{y_1}$ and $\dfrac{x}{x_2}=\dfrac{y}{y_2}$, from which we see that $\dfrac xy=\dfrac{x_1}{y_1}=\dfrac{x_2}{y_2}$
		\item $\dfrac{\nm{AX}}{\nm{BY}}=\dfrac{x_1}{y_2}$ and $\dfrac{\nm{XD}}{\nm{YC}}=\dfrac{x_2}{y_1}$
	\end{enumeratea}
	The result follows by combining these observations and applying the Lemma twice:
	\begin{gather*}
		\begin{aligned}
			\frac{x^2}{y^2}
			&\overset{\text{(a)}}{=}
			\frac{x_1x_2}{y_1y_2}
			\overset{\text{(b)}}{=}
			\frac{\nm{AX}\nm{XD}}{\nm{BY}\nm{YC}}
			\overset{\text{(\ref*{lemm:butterflylemm})}}{=}
			\frac{\nm{PX}\nm{XQ}}{\nm{PY}\nm{YQ}}
			=\frac{(z-x)(z+x)}{(z+y)(z-y)} =\frac{z^2-x^2}{z^2-y^2}
		\end{aligned}\\[2pt]
		\implies x^2(z^2-y^2)=y^2(z^2-x^2)\\
		\implies x^2=y^2\implies x=y\tag*{\qedhere}
	\end{gather*}\vspace{-20pt}
\end{proof}
