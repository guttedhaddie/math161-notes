\section{Fractal Geometry}

\subsection{Natural Geometry, Self-similarity and Fractal Dimension}\label{sec:fracdefn}

The objects of classical geometry (lines, curves, spheres, etc.) seem flatter and less interesting as one zooms in: at small scales, every differentiable curve looks like a line segment! By contrast, real-world objects tend to exhibit greater detail at smaller scales. A seemingly spherical orange is dimpled on closer inspection: is its surface area that of a sphere, or is the area greater due to the dimples? What if we zoom in further? Under a microscope, the dimples are seen to have minute cracks and fissures. With modern technology, we can `see' almost to the molecular level; what does \emph{surface area} even mean at such a scale?

\subsubsection*{The Length of a Coastline} 

In 1967 Benoit Mandelbrot asked a related question in a now-famous paper, \emph{How Long Is the Coast of Britain?\ Statistical Self-Similarity and Fractional Dimension.} His essential point was that this question has no simple answer:\footnote{%
	The official answer from the Ordnance Survey (the UK government's mapping office) is, `It depends.' The all-knowing CIA states 7723 miles, though offers no evidence as to why.%
}
Should we measure by walking along the mean high tide line? Do we include the boundary of every small rock? Do we skirt every grain of sand? Every molecule? As the scale of consideration shrinks, the measured length becomes larger.
\smallbreak

\begin{example}{}{}
	Mandelbrot's approach is little more than the standard method for measuring the arc-length of a curve in calculus. Here it is applied to a smooth circular `coastline.'\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{-\parsep}
		Approximate the path using $N$ rulers of length $R$: clearly
		\[
			R=2\sin\frac\pi N
		\]
		The sum of the ruler-lengths approximates the circumference:
		\[
			NR=2N\sin\frac\pi N\approx 2N\cdot\frac{\pi}N =2\pi
		\]
		where we used the small angle approximation for sine\footnotemark{} as $N\to\infty$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{-\parsep}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={approximating arc-length for a circle using straight rulers}]{self-sim-circle}
	\end{minipage}
	\smallbreak
	Otherwise said, as the length $R$ of the measuring ruler shrinks, the number required to measure the path grows according to
	\[
		N\approx 2\pi R^{-1}
	\]
\end{example}

\footnotetext{%
	This is the familiar fact that $\lim\limits_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}\theta=1$.%
}

There is nothing special about the circle. Measuring any smooth ``calculus'' curve with $N$ small rulers of length $R$, we see that the arc-length is approximately $L\approx NR$ (Exercise \ref{exs:fractaldiffcurve}).
\smallbreak

But what happens if we apply this approach to a non-smooth curve like a coastline\ldots

\begin{defn}{Fractal Dimension of a Curve (empirical)}{mbrotfrac1}
	Given a ruler of length $R$, let $N$ be the number of rulers needed to trace round a curve when laid end-to-end.
	\smallbreak
	Plot $\log N$ against $\log (1/R)$ for several sizes of ruler. The data suggests a straight line:
  \[
  	\log N\approx \log k+D\log(1/R)=\log (kR^{-D})\implies N\approx kR^{-D}
  \]
  The number $D$ is Mandelbrot's \emph{fractal dimension} of the coastline.
\end{defn}

\begin{examples}{}{}
	While this notion of fractal dimension is purely empirical, it does seem to capture something about the `roughness' of a curve.
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item Since a smooth curve with arc-length $L$ satisfies $N\approx LR^{-1}$, any such reassuringly has fractal dimension $D=1$.
	  \item Thanks to its relatively rugged north and west coasts, the island of Great Britain has fractal dimension significantly higher than that of a smooth curve $D\approx 1.25$.
	  \begin{center}
	  	\begin{tabular}{c@{\qquad\qquad}c}
	  		\includegraphics[height=240pt,alt={the coastline of great britain}]{britain.png}
	  		&
	  		\includegraphics[height=240pt,alt={the coastline of norway}]{norway.png}
	  		\\
	  		British Mainland $D\approx 1.25$
	  		&
	  		Norwegian West Coast $D\approx 1.52$
	  	\end{tabular}
	  \end{center}
	  \item With its myriad fjords, Norway has an even rougher coastline,\footnotemark{} and thus a higher fractal dimension $D\approx 1.52$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{examples}

\footnotetext{Ignore the small islands, and the (smooth) eastern border with Sweden and Finland.}

For more detail regarding these coastline examples, see the Fractal Foundation's \href{http://fractalfoundation.org/OFC/OFC-10-4.html}{website.} Mandelbrot coined the word \emph{fractal}, though he didn't invent the concept from nothing. Rather he applied earlier ideas of Hausdorff, Minkowski and others, and observed how the natural world contains many examples of fractal structures.

\subsubsection*{Self-Similar Figures}

Our goal is to describe a related notion of fractional dimension. To help motivate this, recall some of the standard objects of pre-fractal geometry.\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.8\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item A \textcolor{mGreen}{segment} comprises $N$ copies of itself each scaled by a factor $\smash{r=\frac 1N}$.
		\item A \textcolor{Blue}{square} comprises $N$ copies of itself scaled by $\smash{r=\frac 1{\sqrt N}}$.
		\item A cube comprises $N$ copies of itself scaled by $\smash{r=\frac 1{\sqrt[3]{N}}}$.
	\end{itemize}
	In each case $N=r^{-D}$, where $D$ is the usual dimension (1, 2 or 3). Inspired by this, we make a loose definition.
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.18\linewidth}\vspace{-2\parsep}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={a segment is self-similar, as is a square}]{self-sim-line}
\end{minipage}


\begin{defn}{}{selfsim}
	A geometric figure is \emph{self-similar} if it may be subdivided\footnotemark{} into $N$ similar copies of itself, each scaled by a magnification factor $r<1$. The \emph{fractal dimension} of such a figure is
	\[
		D:=\log_{1/r}N=\frac{\log N}{\log (1/r)}=-\frac{\log N}{\log r}
	\]\vspace{-\parsep}
\end{defn}

\footnotetext{%
	In this and what follows, boundary points (e.g., endpoints of sub-segments) can end up in multiple subsets.%
}

\begin{example}{}{}
	The pictures offer some evidence for non-integer fractal dimension, and for the idea that self-similarity is a natural phenomenon. If one ignores the \textcolor{Brown}{stem}, the `tree' comprises $N=3$ copies of itself each scaled by $r=0.4$.
	The fern has $N=7$ and $r=0.3$.
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{c@{\qquad\qquad}c}
			\includegraphics[alt={a fractal mimicking a tree}]{tree2}
			&
			\includegraphics[alt={a fractal mimicking a fern}]{tree3}
			\\
			Fractal Tree $D=-\frac{\log 3}{\log 0.4}\approx 1.20$
			&
			\href{http://www.math.uci.edu/~ndonalds/math161/fern-code.html}{Fractal Fern} $D=-\frac{\log 7}{\log 0.3}\approx 1.62$
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	With dimensions between 1 and 2, both objects exhibit an intuitive idea of fractal dimension: both seem to occupy more space than mere \emph{lines}, but neither has positive \emph{area}. Moreover, the fern seems to occupy more space---has higher dimension---than the tree.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{Cantor's Middle-Third Set}{cantor}
	This famous example dates from the late 1800s.\footnotemark{}\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{-\parsep}
		Starting with the unit interval $C_0=[0,1]$, define a sequence of sets $(C_n)$ where $C_{n+1}$ is obtained by deleting the open `middle-third' of each interval in $C_n$; for instance
		\[
			C_1=\left[0,\frac 13\right]\cup\left[\frac 23,1\right]
		\]
		Cantor's set is essentially the limit of this sequence:
		\[
			\mathcal C:=\bigcap_{n=0}^\infty C_n
		\]
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.38\linewidth}\vspace{-\parsep}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={repeatedly delete the middle third of each segment}]{cantor-set}
	\end{minipage}
	\smallbreak

	Cantor's set has several strange properties, which we consider only non-rigorously.
	\begin{description}
	  \item[Zero length] Since we delete $\frac 13$ of the remaining set at each step, the sum of the lengths of the sub-intervals comprising $C_n$ is $\left(\frac 23\right)^n$. It follows that
	  \[
	  	\forall n\in\N_0,\ \operatorname{length}(\mathcal C)\le \left(\frac 23\right)^n \implies \operatorname{length}(\mathcal C)=0
	  \]
	  We conclude that $\mathcal C$ contains no subintervals!
	  \item[Uncountability] There exists a bijection between $\mathcal C$ and the original interval $[0,1]$. This is of limited interest to us, though you've likely encountered the notion elsewhere.
	  \item[Self-similarity] $C_{n+1}$ comprises two copies of $C_n$, each shrunk by a factor of $\frac 13$. Abusing notation,
		\[
			C_{n+1}=\frac 13C_n\cup\left(\frac 13C_n+\frac 23\right) 
			\xrightarrow[\text{`Take Limits'}]{}
			\mathcal C=\frac 13\mathcal C\cup\left(\frac 13\mathcal C+\frac 23\right)
		\]
		Cantor's set is seen to comprise 
	\href{http://www.math.uci.edu/~ndonalds/math161/cantor-similar.html}{two shrunken copies of itself}, and thus has fractal dimension $D=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}\approx 0.63$.
	\end{description}

	Cantor's set has many generalizations:
	\begin{itemize}
	  \item Removing other fractions of each interval produces Cantor-type sets with other fractal dimensions. For instance, removing the 2\nd{} and 4\th{} fifths results in $D=\frac{\log 3}{\log 5}\approx 0.68$.
	  \item Higher-dimensional analogues include the \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpiński_triangle}{Sierpiński triangle} ($D=\smash[t]{\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}}\approx 1.59$) and carpet (Example \ref*{ex:scarpet}.\ref{ex:scarpet2}, $D=\frac{\log 8}{\log 3}\approx 1.89$), and the Menger sponge ($D=\frac{\log 20}{\log 3}\approx 2.73$).
	\end{itemize}
\end{example}


\footnotetext{%
	While the set is named for Cantor, it was first described during Henry Smith's 1874 investigations of integrability: this relates to the `length' of a set, which was later formalized using \emph{measure theory}.%
}


\begin{example}{The Koch Curve and Snowflake}{koch}
	Another generalization of the Cantor set is produced as the limit of a sequence of curves.\par

		\begin{itemize}
		  \item Let $K_0$ be a segment of length 1.
		  \item Create $K_1$ by replacing the middle third of $K_0$ with the remaining sides of an upward-pointing equilateral triangle.
		  \item Replace the middle third of each segment in $K_1$ as before to create $K_2$.
		  \item Repeat \emph{ad infinitum.}
		\end{itemize}
		The resulting \href{http://www.math.uci.edu/~ndonalds/math161/koch-anim.html}{curve} is drawn. The \emph{Koch snowflake} is obtained by arranging three copies around an equilateral triangle.

	\begin{center}
		\begin{minipage}[b]{0.45\linewidth}\vspace{-\parsep}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[alt={the fractal curve}]{koch}\\
			Koch Curve\\[18.5pt]
			\includegraphics[alt={self-similarity. The full curve comprises four shrunken copies of the whole}]{koch3}\\
			Self-similarity
		\end{minipage}
		\qquad
		\begin{minipage}[b]{0.4\linewidth}\vspace{-\parsep}
			\centering
			\includegraphics[alt={assemble three copies of the curve to produce a snowflake}]{koch2}\\
			Koch Snowflake
		\end{minipage}
	\end{center}
		
		The relation to the Cantor set should be obvious in the construction. Indeed if we visualize $K_0$ as the interval $[0,1]$, then the intersection of $K_0$ with the Koch curve is the Cantor set itself!
		\smallbreak
		
		The Koch curve is self-similar in that it comprises $N=4$ copies of itself, each shrunk by a factor of $r=\frac 13$. Its fractal dimension is therefore
		\[
			D=\frac{\log 4}{\log 3}\approx 1.26
		\]		
		We may also consider the curve's length. Let $N_n$ be the number of segments in $K_n$, each having length $R_n$, then $\ell_n=N_nR_n$ is the length of the curve $K_n$. It follows that
		\[
		  N_n=4^n,\quad R_n=\frac 1{3^n}
		  \implies \ell_n=\left(\frac 43\right)^n\to\infty
		\]
		The Koch curve is \emph{infinitely long}! Consider also how this construction fits with Mandelbrot's discussion of the fractal dimension of a coastline (Definition \ref{defn:mbrotfrac1}): measuring the Koch curve using $N_n$ rulers of length $R_n$, we see that
		\[
			\log N_n=n\log 4=-\frac{\log 4}{\log 3}\log R_n \implies N_n=R_n^{-D}
		\]
\end{example}

\subsection{Contraction Mappings \& Iterated Function Systems}

Thus far we have dealt informally with fractals in which the whole consists of multiple pieces scaled by the same factor. To add more variety, we'd like to consider multiple scale factors. To do this, and to be more rigorous, we need to borrow some ideas from topology and analysis.

\begin{defn}{}{}
	A \emph{contraction mapping} with scale factor $c\in[0,1)$ is a function $S:\R^m\to\R^m$ such that
	\[
		\forall x,y\in\R^m,\ \ \nm{S(x)-S(y)}\le c\nm{x-y}
	\]
\end{defn}

A contraction mapping moves inputs closer together. It should be clear that every contraction mapping is continuous ($\lim x_n=y\Longrightarrow \lim S(x_n)=S(y)$).

\begin{example}{}{banacheasy}
	The function $S:\R\to\R:x\mapsto \frac 13x+\frac 23$ is a contraction mapping with scale factor $c=\frac 13$: indeed
	\[
		\nm{S(x)-S(y)}=\frac 13\nm{x-y}
	\]
	To motivate the key theorem, consider using $S$ to inductively define a sequence: given any $x_0$, define
	\[
		x_{n+1}:=S(x_n)
	\]
	The first few terms are
	\[
		x_1=\frac{x_0}3+\frac 23,\qquad x_2=\frac{x_0}{3^2}+\frac 2{3^2}+\frac 23,\qquad x_3=\frac{x_0}{3^3}+\frac 2{3^3}+\frac 2{3^2}+\frac 23
	\]
	which suggests (geometric series)
	\[
		x_n=\frac{x_0}{3^n}+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac 1{3^k} 
		=\frac{x_0}{3^n}+\frac{2(3^{-1}-3^{-n-1})}{1-3^{-1}}
		=1+\frac 1{3^n}(x_0-1)
	\]
	This can easily be verified by induction if you prefer. The striking thing about this sequence is that it converges to the same limit $\lim x_n=1$ \emph{regardless of the initial term $x_0$}!
\end{example}

The example illustrates one of the most powerful and useful theorems in mathematics.

\begin{thm}{Banach Fixed Point Theorem}{banach}
	Let $S:\R\to\R$ be a contraction mapping. Then:
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item $S$ has a unique \textbf{fixed point}: some $L\in\R^m$ such that $S(L)=L$.
	  \item If $x_0\in\R$ is \textbf{any} value, then the sequence defined iteratively by $x_{n+1}:=S(x_n)$ converges to $L$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

If you're comfortable with analysis, the proof is straightforward (Exercise \ref{exs:banach}).\smallbreak
In fact, as will be crucial momentarily, Banach's result holds whenever $S:\mathcal H\to\mathcal H$ is a contraction mapping on any \emph{complete metric space}.\footnote{%
	Very loosely, a metric space is a set on which a sensible notion of \emph{distance} can be defined: on Euclidean $\R^2$, for instance, the distance between two points is $d(x,y)=\sqrt{(x_1-y_1)^2+(x_2-y_2)^2}$. If you've studied analysis you'll be familiar with \emph{completeness}: every Cauchy sequence converges (in $\mathcal H$).%
}
Our goal is to use Banach's result to generate certain types of fractal via repeated applications of contraction mappings to an initial shape. Our motivating example already illustrates this.


\begin{example*}{\ref{ex:cantor}, Cantor Set mk.\,II}{}
	The functions $S_1,S_2:\R\to\R$ where
	\[
		S_1(x)=\frac x3\qquad S_2(x)=\frac x3+\frac 23
	\]
	are contraction mappings with scale factor $c=\frac 13$. More importantly, these functions \emph{define} the Cantor set: at each stage of its construction, we defined
	\[
		C_{n+1}:=S_1(C_n)\cup S_2(C_n) \tag{$\ast$}
	\]
	The self-similarity of the Cantor set can be expressed in the same manner: $\mathcal C=S_1(\mathcal C)\cup S_2(\mathcal C)$.
	\medbreak
	
	Amazingly, it barely seems to matter what initial set $C_0$ we choose. Originally, we took $C_0=[0,1]$ to be the unit interval, but we could instead start with the singleton set $C_0=\{0\}$: in this case, iterating ($\ast$) produces
	\[
		C_1=\big\{0,\tfrac 23\big\},\quad 
		C_2=\big\{0,\tfrac 29,\tfrac 23,\tfrac 89\big\},\quad 
		C_3=\big\{0,\tfrac 2{27},\tfrac 29,\tfrac 8{27},\tfrac 23,\tfrac{20}{27},\tfrac 89,\tfrac{26}{27}\big\},\quad \ldots
	\]
	The first few iterations are drawn in the first picture below; it appears as if, in the limit, $C_n$ is `approaching' the Cantor set.
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{c@{\qquad\qquad}c}
			\includegraphics[alt={The cantor set generated from a single point}]{cantor-similar3}
			&
			\includegraphics[alt={The cantor set generated from two intervals}]{cantor-similar2}
			\\[5pt]
			Initial set $C_0=\{0\}$
			&
			Initial set $C_0=[\frac 2{10},\frac 5{10}]\cup[\frac 6{10},\frac 7{10}]$
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	The second picture starts with a very different initial set $C_0=[0.2,0.5]\cup[0.6,0.7]$; iterating this also appears to produce the Cantor set!
\end{example*}

\subsubsection*{Iterated Function Systems}

It certainly appears as if the Cantor set is generated by the contraction maps $S_1,S_2$ independently of the initial data $C_0$. Our main result shows in what sense this is true. Since this requires some heavy lifting from topology and analysis, we provide only a synopsis.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A subset $K\subset\R^m$ is \emph{compact} if it is \emph{closed} (contains its boundary points) and \emph{bounded} ($K$ lies within some ball centered at the origin). For instance:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The closed interval $K=[0,1]$ is a compact subset of $\R$.
    \item The open disk $D=\big\{(x,y):x^2+y^2<1\big\}$ is a non-compact subset of $\R^2$, since it does not contain its boundary points (the unit circle).
  \end{itemize}
  
  \item The set of non-empty compact subsets of $\R^m$ is a \emph{metric space} $\mathcal H$. This means that the \emph{distance} $d(X,Y)$ between $X,Y\in\mathcal H$ may sensibly be defined, though it is a little tricky\ldots\par
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.68\linewidth}\vspace{-\parsep}
  	The distance function is the \emph{Hausdorff metric.} Given $Y\in\mathcal H$ and $x\in \R^m$, define $\mathcolor{mRed}{d_Y(x)}=\inf_{y\in Y}\Nm{x-y}$ to be the distance from $x$ to the `nearest' point of $Y$. Define $\mathcolor{Blue}{d_X(y)}$ similarly. The distance between $X$ and $Y$ is then
  	\[
  		d(X,Y):=\max\left\{\sup_{x\in X}d_Y(x),\sup_{y\in Y}d_X(y)\right\}
  	\]
  \end{minipage}
  \hfill
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.31\linewidth}\vspace{-1.5\parsep}
  	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={the distance between two compact sets}]{hausdorff}
  \end{minipage}
  \smallbreak
  
  Roughly speaking, find $x\in X$ which is as far away as possible from anything in $Y$, and find $y\in Y$ similarly; $d(X,Y)$ is the larger of these distances. Crucially, for compact sets, $d(X,Y)=0\Longleftrightarrow X=Y$.
  
  \item Since $\mathcal H$ is a metric space, we can discuss convergent sequences $(K_n)$ of compact sets
	\[
		\lim_{n\to\infty}K_n=K\iff \lim_{n\to\infty}d(K_n,K)=0
	\]
	It also makes sense to speak of Cauchy sequences in $\mathcal H$. It may be proved that $\mathcal H$ is \emph{complete}: every Cauchy sequence $(K_n)\subseteq\mathcal H$ converges to some $K\in\mathcal H$.
	
  \item The main result is a corollary of Banach's Theorem (\ref{thm:banach}).
\end{itemize}


\begin{thm}{}{ifs}
	Let $S_1,\ldots,S_n$ be contraction mappings on $\R^m$ with scale factors $c_1,\ldots,c_n$. Define
	\[
		S:\mathcal H\to\mathcal H
		\quad\text{by}\quad 
		S(K)=\bigcup_{i=1}^nS_i(K)
	\]
	\vspace{-\parsep}
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item $S$ is a contraction mapping on $\mathcal H$ with scale factor $c=\max(c_1,\ldots,c_n)$.
	  \item $S$ has a unique fixed set $F\in\mathcal H$ given by $F=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty} S^k(K_0)$ for \textbf{any} non-empty $K_0\in\mathcal H$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

Part 1 is not super difficult to prove if you are willing to grapple with the Hausdorff metric (try it if you're comfortable with analysis). Part 2 is Banach's theorem.\smallbreak

The upshot is this: repeatedly applying contraction mappings to \emph{any} non-empty compact set $K_0$ produces a compact limit set which is \emph{independent of $K_0$!} We call this limit $F$ for \emph{fractal.} Such fractals are sometimes called \emph{attractors}: being limit-sets, they `attract' data towards themselves.


\begin{examples}{}{scarpet}
	We consider four iterated function systems.

	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item (Example\,\ref{ex:cantor}, mk.III)\lstsp The contractions $S_1(x)=\frac 13x$ and $S_2(x)=\frac 13x+\frac 23$ (on $\R$) produce a contraction map $S:\mathcal H\to\mathcal H$:
		\[
			S(K):=\bigl\{S_1(x),S_2(x):x\in K\bigr\}
		\]
		By Theorem \ref{thm:ifs}, if $C_0\subset\R$ is non-empty, closed and bounded, then  $\mathcal C=\lim S^n(C_0)$. Certainly all three of our previous choices for $C_0$ are such sets, namely $[0,1]$, $\{0\}$ and $[0.2,0.5]\cup[0.6,0.7]$.
		\medbreak
		
		A fun application of the Theorem allows us to find all sorts of interesting points in the Cantor set. For instance, consider the compositions $T$ and $U$, where
		\[
			T(x)=S_1\bigl(S_2(x)\bigr)=\frac x9+\frac 29
			\quad\text{and}\quad
			U(y)=S_2\bigl(S_1(y)\bigr)=\frac y9+\frac 23
		\]
		These are both contraction maps on $\R$ ($c=\frac 19$) whose unique fixed points are $\mathcolor{mRed}{t=\frac 14}$ and $\mathcolor{Blue}{u=\frac 34}$; moreover $S_1(u)=t$ and $S_2(t)=u$. Now consider the non-empty compact set $K=\{\mathcolor{mRed}{t},\mathcolor{Blue}{u}\}\in\mathcal H$. Plainly\par
		\begin{minipage}[t]{0.5\linewidth}\vspace{-14pt}
			\begin{align*}

 		\smallbreak
 		At first glance, this seems paradoxical. We know that $\mathcal C$ contains no intervals. Natural intuition from our initial construction therefore suggests that $\mathcal C$ contains only the endpoints of deleted open intervals. But such endpoints must have denominator $3^n$, so what is $\smash{\frac 14}$ doing there?!
 		\smallbreak
 		The problem isn't the Cantor set, our understanding and intuition with regard to the real numbers is simply weak. Indeed, $\mathcal C$ is \emph{uncountably} infinite, whereas there are only \emph{countably} many points in the interval $[0,1]$ with denominator $3^n$, so \emph{almost all} points in $\mathcal C$ are other than the endpoint of a deleted interval.

\subsubsection*{Fractal Dimension Revisited}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.8\linewidth}\vspace{-2\parsep}
	Since Theorem \ref{thm:ifs} permits several different contraction factors, we need a new approach to fractal dimension. We ask how many disks of a given radius $\epsilon$ are required to cover a set.
	\smallbreak
	In the picture, the \textcolor{Blue}{unit square} requires four disks of radius $\varepsilon=0.4$. For smaller $\varepsilon$, we plainly need more disks\ldots
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.19\linewidth}\vspace{-2\parsep}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={covering a square with four disks}]{epsiloncover}
\end{minipage}


\begin{defn}{}{}
	Let $K$ be a compact subset of $\R^m$.
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item If $\varepsilon>0$, the \emph{closed $\varepsilon$-ball centered at $x\in K$} consists of the points lying at most a distance $\varepsilon$ from $x$:
		\[
			B_\epsilon(x)=\big\{y\in\R^m:d(x,y)\le\varepsilon\big\}
		\]
		\item The \emph{minimal $\varepsilon$-covering number} for $K$ is the smallest number of radius-$\varepsilon$ balls needed to cover $K$:
		\[
			\mathcal N(K,\varepsilon)=\min\left\{M:\exists x_1,\ldots,x_M\in K\text{ with }K\subseteq \bigcup\limits_{n=1}^MB_\varepsilon(x_n)\right\}
		\]
		\item The \emph{fractal dimension} of $K$ is the limit
		\[
			D=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log\mathcal N(K,\varepsilon)}{\log (1/\varepsilon)}
		\]
		\vspace{-\parsep}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

Rigorously proving that $\cN$ and $D$ exist requires a more thorough study of topology, though a simple example should at least convince us that the definition is reasonable.

\begin{example}{}{intervalcover}
	Let $K=[0,1]$ be the interval of length 1. It is not hard to check that
	\[
		\varepsilon\ge\frac 12\iff \cN(K,\varepsilon)=1\quad\text{and}\quad 
		\frac 14\le \varepsilon<\frac 12\iff \cN(K,\varepsilon)=2
	\]
	etc. More generally, any positive integer $\cN$ is related to $\epsilon$ via
	\[
		\frac 1{2\cN}\le\epsilon<\frac 1{2(\cN-1)}
	\]
	% \[\def\arraystretch{1.3}\begin{array}{c|c}
	% \text{range of }\varepsilon&\mathcal N([0,1],\epsilon)\\\hline
	% \frac 12\le \varepsilon&1\\
	% \frac 14\le\varepsilon<\frac 12&2\\
	% \frac 16\le\varepsilon<\frac 14&3\\
	% \frac 18\le\varepsilon<\frac 16&4\\
	% \frac 1{10}\le\varepsilon<\frac 18&5\\
	% \frac 1{2m}\le\varepsilon<\frac 1{2(m-1)}&m
	% \end{array}\]
	% \[\begin{array}{c|ccccccc}\def\arraystretch{1.3}
	% \text{range of }\varepsilon & [\frac 1{2m},\frac 1{2(m-1)}) & \cdots & [\frac 1{10},\frac 18) & [\frac 18,\frac 16) & [\frac 16,\frac 14) & [\frac 14,\frac 12) & [\frac 12,\infty)\\\hline
	% \mathcal N([0,1],\epsilon) & m & \cdots & 5 & 4 & 3 & 2 & 1
	% \end{array}\]
	The fractal dimension of $K$ ($=1$) may be recovered via the squeeze theorem
	\[%\frac{\log\mathcal N}{\log (2\cN)} <\frac{\log\mathcal N}{\log (1/\varepsilon)}< \frac{\log\mathcal N}{\log (2(\cN-1))}  \implies 
		D=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log\mathcal N}{\log (1/\varepsilon)}=1
	\]
	The details are left to Exercise \ref{exs:intervalcover}.
\end{example}


\pagebreak[3]


Thankfully, an easier-to-visualize modification is available using boxes.

\begin{thm}{Box-counting}{}
	Let $K\subset\R^m$ be compact and cover $\R^m$ by boxes of side length $\frac 1{2^n}$. Let $\mathcal N_n(K)$ be the number of such boxes intersecting $K$. Then
	\[
		D=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathcal N_n(K)}{\log 2^n}
	\]
	\vspace{-\parsep}
\end{thm}





Our last result is stated without proof: a formula satisfied by the dimension of an iterated function system (Theorem \ref{thm:ifs}).

\begin{thm}{}{ifsdimension}
	Let $\big\{S_n\big\}_{n=1}^M$ be an iterated function system with attractor (limiting fractal) $F$ and where each contraction $S_n$ has scale factor $c_n\in(0,1)$. Under reasonable conditions,\footnotemark{} its fractal dimension is the unique real number $D$ satisfying
	\[
		\sum_{n=1}^Mc_n^D=1
	\]
	\vspace{-\parsep}
\end{thm}

\footnotetext{%
	Roughly: the outputs of each $S_n$ applied to $F$ meet only at boundary points; the `pieces' of the fractal cannot overlap too much.%
}

The existence and uniqueness of $D$ is proved in Exercise \ref{exs:ifsdimproof}. When the scale-factors are identical ($c_n=r$), we easily recover Definition \ref{defn:selfsim}:
\[
	Mr^D=1\implies D=\frac{-\log M}{\log r}=\frac{\log M}{\log (1/r)}
\]


\begin{examples}{}{}
	In more general cases, the Theorem is usually impossible to use \emph{algebraically}; a numerical approximation for $D$ is required.
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item The fractal fern (Examples \ref*{ex:scarpet}.\ref{ex:fracfern}) is generated by three contraction maps with scale factors $\smash{\frac 34,\frac 14,\frac 14}$. Its dimension is the solution to the equation
	  \[
	  	\left(\frac 34\right)^D+\left(\frac 14\right)^D+\left(\frac 14\right)^D=1\implies D\approx 1.327
	  \]
	  \item In extreme cases, $D$ may be computed exactly. For instance, if an iterated function system comprises five contraction maps with scale factors $\smash{c_1=c_2=\frac 12}$ and $\smash{c_3=c_4=c_5=\frac 14}$, then the dimension of resulting fractal satisfies
	  \[
	  	2\left(\frac 12\right)^D+3\left(\frac 14\right)^D=1
	  \]
	  This is quadratic in $\alpha=\left(\frac 12\right)^D$, whence
	  \[
	  	2\alpha+3\alpha^2=1\implies \alpha=\frac 13\implies D=\log_23\approx 1.58
	  \]
	\end{enumerate}
\end{examples}

\subsubsection*{Other methods of creating fractals}

It is hard to give a rigorous definition of \emph{fractal}, though (as with iterated function systems) they typically arise from the repeated application of some function or procedure. Two other famous examples where iterations produce a fractal are the \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logistic_map}{\emph{logistic map}} and the \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandelbrot_set}{\emph{Mandelbrot set}}.
\smallbreak

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.62\linewidth}\vspace{-\parsep}
	The Mandelbrot set (pictured) arises from a construction best described in the complex plane. Given $c\in\C$, iterate the function
	\[
		f_c(z)=z^2+c
	\]
	If $\smash{f\Big(f\big(f(\cdots f(c)\cdots)\big)\Big)}$ remains bounded, no matter how many times $f$ is applied, then $c$ lies in the Mandelbrot set.
	\smallbreak
	Far better pictures (and trippy videos) can be found online: sit back and let geometry scramble your conception of reality\ldots
