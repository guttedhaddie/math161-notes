\section{Hyperbolic Geometry}\label{chap:hyper}

\subsection{History: Saccheri, Lambert and Absolute Geometry}\label{sec:hyp1}

For 2000 years after Euclid, many mathematicians believed that his parallel postulate (page \pageref{pg:parallelpost}) could not be an independent axiom. Much effort was expended trying to demonstrate this, not least by Giovanni Saccheri (1667--1733) \& Johann Lambert (1728--1777); both attempted to force contradictions by assuming the negation of the parallel postulate. While this approach ultimately failed, their insights provided the foundation of a new \emph{non-Euclidean} geometry.


\subsubsection*{Absolute and Non-Euclidean Geometry}

Before considering the work of Saccheri and Lambert, we define some terms and recall our earlier discussion of parallels (pages \pageref{pg:parallelexist}--\pageref{pg:pythagoras}).

\begin{defn}{}{}
	\emph{Absolute} or \emph{neutral} geometry is the axiomatic system comprising all of Hilbert's axioms except Playfair (page \pageref{pg:hilbertaxioms}). Euclidean geometry is a special case of absolute geometry.\smallbreak
	A \emph{non-Euclidean geometry} is (loosely) a model satisfying most of Hilbert's axioms but for which parallels might not exist or be unique: 
		\begin{quote}
		  There exists a line $\ell$ and a point $P$ not on $\ell$ which lies on \emph{no parallel} or \emph{at least two parallel lines} to $\ell$. 
		\end{quote}
\end{defn}

Spherical geometry (page \pageref{pg:sphere}) is non-Euclidean since there are no parallel lines: Hilbert's axioms I-2 and O-3 are false, as is the exterior angle theorem.


\paragraph{Results in absolute geometry}

The conclusions of Euclid's first 28 theorems are valid.\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{-2pt}
	\begin{itemize}
	  \item Basic constructions: midpoint, bisectors, perpendiculars, etc.
	  \item Triangle congruence theorems: SAS, ASA, SAA, SSS.
 	\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{-7pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={existence of a parallel through a point not on a line}]{history-playfair}
\end{minipage}\vspace{-18pt}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Exterior angle theorem and its consequences:
	  \begin{itemize}
	  \item Side-angle comparison and the triangle inequality (Exercise \ref*{sec:hilbert2}.\ref{ex:euclidi19}).
	  \item \emph{Existence} of a parallel $m$ to a line $\ell$ through a point $P\not\in\ell$: as pictured $\alpha\cong\beta\Longrightarrow\ell\parallel m$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}


\paragraph{Arguments making use of unique parallels}\label{pg:absolute}

Since the following were proved using Playfair's axiom or the parallel postulate, their arguments are false in absolute geometry:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A line crossing parallel lines makes congruent angles: in the picture,  $\ell\parallel m\Longrightarrow \alpha\cong\beta$. This is the \emph{uniqueness} claim in Playfair: the parallel $m$ to $\ell$ through $P$ is unique.
  \item Angles in a triangle sum to a straight-edge (\ang{180}).
  \item Constructions of squares/rectangles.
  \item Pythagoras' Theorem.
\end{itemize}

\pagebreak[3]

While our \emph{arguments} for the above are false in absolute geometry, we cannot immediately claim that the \emph{results} are also; there might be alternative proofs! To see that these results truly require unique parallels, we must exhibit a \emph{model} of absolute geometry in which they are false.\footnote{
	The model must be of \emph{absolute} geometry. For instance, the angles in a spherical triangle sum to more than \ang{180}, but we can't guarantee that the reason for this is a \emph{only} the lack of parallels, since spherical geometry fails two of Hilbert's other axioms.  
}
Developing such a model is the primary purpose of this chapter. Its existence explains why Saccheri and Lambert ultimate goal was unrealized: the parallel postulate (Playfair) is indeed independent of Euclid's (Hilbert's) other axioms.


\subsubsection*{The Saccheri--Legendre Theorem}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.57\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
	We turn now to Saccheri's consideration of triangles, suitably modernized. We work in absolute geometry, starting with an extension of Euclid's proof of the exterior angle theorem.\smallbreak
	Suppose $\triangle ABC$ has angle-sum $\Sigma_{\triangle}$, let $\alpha_0=m\angle ABC$ and construct $M$ and $E$ following Euclid. Observe:
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.41\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={the exterior angle theorem set-up}]{history-saccheri}
\end{minipage}

\begin{enumerate}
  \def\lgbullet#1{\raisebox{-2pt}{\textcolor{#1}{\LARGE\textbullet}}}
  \item $\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle ACB} +\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle CAB} \cong\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle ACB} +\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle ACE}<\ang{180}$ is the exterior angle theorem. More generally, the exterior angle theorem guarantees that the \emph{sum of any two angles in a triangle is strictly less than \ang{180}}.
  
  \item $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle EBC$ have the \emph{same angle-sum}---just look at the picture!
  \[
  	\Sigma_{\triangle}=\lgbullet{Brown} +\lgbullet{mGreen} +\lgbullet{Blue} +\lgbullet{mMagenta}
  \]
  Remember that we do not know whether $\Sigma_{\triangle}=\ang{180}$!
  
  \item $\triangle EBC$ has at least one angle ($\mathcolor{Blue}{\angle EBC}$ or $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\angle BEC}$) measuring at most $\frac 12\alpha_0=\frac 12m\angle ABC$.
\end{enumerate}

Iterate this construction: if $\mathcolor{Blue}{\angle EBC}$ is smaller, start by bisecting $\cl{CE}$; otherwise bisect $\cl{BC}\ldots$.\par
The result is an infinite sequence of triangles $\triangle_1=\triangle EBC$, $\triangle_2$, $\triangle_3,\ldots$ with two properties:
\begin{enumeratea}
  \item All triangles in the sequence have \emph{same angle-sum} $\Sigma_{\triangle}=\Sigma_{\triangle_1}=\Sigma_{\triangle_2}=\cdots$.
  \item The $n\th$ triangle $\triangle_n$ has at least one angle measuring $\alpha_n\le\frac 1{2^n}\alpha_0$.
\end{enumeratea} 

Now suppose, for contradiction, that $\Sigma_{\triangle}=\ang{180}+\epsilon$ is \emph{strictly greater} than \ang{180}. Since $\lim\frac 1{2^n}=0$, we may choose $n$ large enough to guarantee $\alpha_n<\epsilon$:
\[
	n>\log_2\frac{\alpha_0}{\epsilon} \implies \alpha_n<\epsilon
\]
But then the sum of the \emph{other two} angles in $\triangle_n$ would be \emph{greater than} \ang{180}, contradicting the exterior angle theorem (observation 1)! We have proved a famous result.

\begin{thm}{Saccheri--Legendre}{saccleg}
	Triangles in absolute geometry have angle-sum $\Sigma_{\triangle}\le \ang{180}$.
\end{thm}

Saccheri's failed hope was to prove \emph{equality} without invoking the parallel postulate.

\subsubsection*{Saccheri and Lambert Quadrilaterals}

Two families of quadrilaterals in absolute geometry are named in honor of these pioneers.

\begin{defn}{}{}
	In absolute geometry, a \emph{Lambert quadrilateral} $AMND$ has three right-angles.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{-2pt}
		A \emph{Saccheri quadrilateral} $ABCD$ satisfies
		\[
			\cl{AD}\cong\cl{BC}\quad\text{and}\quad \measuredangle DAB=\measuredangle CBA=\ang{90}
		\]
		$\cl{AB}$ is the \emph{base} and $\cl{CD}$ the \emph{summit.} The interior angles at $C$ and $D$ are the \emph{summit angles.}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.35\linewidth}\vspace{-2pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={two special types of quadrilateral}]{history-quad3}
	\end{minipage}
\end{defn}

We draw these with curved sides to indicate that the summit angles need not be right-angles, though we haven't yet exhibited a model which shows they could be anything else. Regardless of how they are drawn, $\cl{AD}$, $\cl{BC}$ and $\cl{CD}$ are all \emph{segments}!

\begin{lemm}{}{saccheribasic}
	Saccheri and Lambert quadrilaterals exhibit basic symmetry as pictured.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.64\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{enumerate}
	  	\item If the base and summit of a Saccheri quadrilateral are bisected, we obtain two congruent Lambert quadrilaterals.
	  	\item The summit angles of a Saccheri quadrilateral are congruent.
			\item In \textbf{Euclidean geometry}, Saccheri and Lambert quadrilaterals are \textbf{rectangles}: the summit angles are also right-angles, and the summit and base are congruent.
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.35\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={symmetry of special quadrilaterals}]{history-quad4}
	\end{minipage}
\end{lemm}

We could interpret part 3 as saying that Saccheri and Lambert quadrilaterals are as close as we can get to rectangles in absolute geometry.	The Lemma is mostly a recapitulation of Euclid's Book I construction of a square (Thm. I.\,46, Exercise \ref*{sec:euclid}.\ref{exs:pythagexs}). We leave parts 1 and 2 as exercises: remember that parts 1 and 2 must be proved without reference to Playfair's Axiom!.

\begin{proof}[Proof of part 3]
	Given a Lambert quadrilateral, a second congruent copy forms a Saccheri quadrilateral: if this last is a rectangle, so is the former. It suffices therefore to prove the result for Saccheri quadrilaterals.\smallbreak
	Suppose $ABCD$ is a Saccheri quadrilateral with base $\cl{AB}$. Following the exterior angle theorem, since $\lin{AB}$ is a crossing line making congruent right-angles we conclude that $\lin{AD}\parallel\lin{BC}$.\smallbreak
	The extended summit $\lin{CD}$ also crosses the same parallels. By the parallel postulate, the summit angles sum to a straight-edge. Since these are congruent (part 2), both are right-angles.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.64\linewidth}\vspace{-2pt}
		Finally, join $\cl{AC}$. Again by the parallel postulate, $\lin{AD}\parallel\lin{BC}$ says the alternate angles are congruent ($\mathcolor{BlueViolet}{\angle DAC\cong\angle BCA}$). By SAS the triangles are congruent ($\triangle DAC\cong\triangle BCA$); in particular so also are the summit and base ($\cl{AB}\cong\cl{CD}$) of the original quadrilateral.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.35\linewidth}\vspace{-4pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={the summit is congruent to the base in Euclidean geometry}]{history-quad5}
	\end{minipage}
\end{proof}

\pagebreak[3]

We now show that drawing a Saccheri quadrilateral with \emph{non-obtuse} summit angles is justified.

\begin{thm}{}{summit90}
	In absolute geometry, the summit angles of a Saccheri quadrilateral measure $\le \ang{90}$. 
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	Suppose $ABCD$ is a Saccheri quadrilateral with base $\cl{AB}$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.65\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		Extend $\cl{CB}$ to $E$ such that $\cl{BE}\cong\cl{DA}$.\smallbreak
		Let $M$ be the midpoint of $\cl{AB}$ and join to $D$ and $E$.\smallbreak
		SAS implies $\angle DAM\cong \triangle EBM$. In particular, the \textcolor{mMagenta}{vertical angles} at $M$ are congruent, whence $M$ lies on $\cl{DE}$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.34\linewidth}\vspace{-25pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={Convert the quadrilateral into a triangle}]{history-quad2}
	\end{minipage}\par
	By the Saccheri--Legendre Theorem, the (congruent) \textcolor{Brown}{summit angles} at $C$ and $D$ sum to
	\begin{align*}	
		\angle ADC +\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle DCB} 
		&=\mathcolor{mGreen}{\angle ADM} +\mathcolor{Blue}{\angle EDC} +\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle DCE}\\
		&=\mathcolor{mGreen}{\angle CED} +\mathcolor{Blue}{\angle EDC} +\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle DCE}\\
		&\le \ang{180} \tag*{\qedhere}
	\end{align*}
	\vspace{-20pt}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Models of Hyperbolic Geometry}\label{sec:hyp-models}

In the 1820-30s, János Bolyai, Carl Friedrich Gauss and Nikolai Lobachevsky independently took the next step.\footnote{%
	Bolyai is the source of the term `absolute geometry.'%
}
Rather than attempting to establish the parallel postulate as a theorem within Euclidean geometry, they defined a new geometry based on an alternative axiom of parallels.

\begin{axiom}{Bolyai--Lobachevsky/Hyperbolic Postulate}{}
	Given a line $\ell$ and a point $P\not\in\ell$, there exist \emph{at least two} parallel lines to $\ell$ through $P$.
\end{axiom}

\emph{Hyperbolic Geometry} is the resulting axiomatic system: Hilbert with Playfair's axiom replaced by the hyperbolic postulate. Consistency was proved in the late 1800s by Beltrami, Klein and Poincaré, each of whom created models by defining \emph{point}, \emph{line}, etc., in novel ways.

\subsubsection*{The Poincaré Disk Model}

One of the simplest models is named for Poincaré, though it was first proposed by Beltrami.\footnote{%
	The key results of hyperbolic geometry can be discussed synthetically without reference to a model, though such an approach would be ahistorical, and masochistic for a first exposure!
}

\begin{defn}{}{poincaredisk}
	The \emph{Poincaré disk} is the interior of the unit circle\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
		\[
			\big\{(x,y)\in\R^2:x^2+y^2<1\big\}
			\quad\text{or}\quad
			\big\{z\in\C:\nm z<1\big\}
		\]
		A \emph{hyperbolic line} is either a diameter or a circular arc meeting the unit circle at right-angles.\smallbreak
		The picture shows a \textcolor{dRed}{hyperbolic line $\ell$} and a \textcolor{Blue}{point $P$}: also drawn are several \textcolor{dGreen}{parallel hyperbolic lines} to $\ell$ passing through $P$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{-22pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={parallel lines in the model}]{models-parallels}
	\end{minipage}\par
	Points on the boundary circle are termed \emph{omega-points}: these are \emph{not} in the Poincaré disk and are essentially `points at infinity.' 
\end{defn}

By the incidence axioms, there exists a unique hyperbolic line joining any two points in the Poincaré disk. Such may straightforwardly be described using equations in analytic geometry.

\begin{lemm}{}{hypline}
	A hyperbolic line in the Poincaré disk model is either:\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.64\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{enumerate}
		  \item A \textcolor{dMagenta}{diameter} with Euclidean equation $dx=cy$ (passes through a point $(c,d)\neq (0,0)$).
		  \item An \textcolor{Blue}{arc} of a Euclidean circle with equation
			\[
				x^2+y^2-2ax-2by+1=0\ \text{ where }\ a^2+b^2>1
			\]
			Euclidean center $C=(a,b)$ and radius $r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2-1}$.
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.35\linewidth}\vspace{-20pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={finding the Euclidean equation of a hyperbolic line}]{models-example}
	\end{minipage}			
\end{lemm}




\begin{example}{}{}
	We compute the equation of the hyperbolic line through the points $P=(\frac 13,\frac 12)$ and $Q=(\frac 12,0)$ in the Poincaré disk: this is the picture shown in Lemma \ref{lemm:hypline}.\smallbreak
	This requires only high-school algebra! Substitute the given points into the equation for a hyperbolic line ($x^2+y^2-2ax-2by+1=0$) to obtain equations for the coefficients $a,b$:
	\[
		\begin{cases}
			\frac 19+\frac 14-\frac 23a-b+1=0\\
			\frac 14-a+1=0
		\end{cases}
		\implies (a,b)=\left(\frac 54,\frac{19}{36}\right)
	\]
	The required hyperbolic line $\lin{PQ}$ therefore has equation
	\[
		x^2+y^2-\frac 52x-\frac{19}{18}y+1=0
		\quad\text{or}\quad
		\left(x-\frac 54\right)^2+\left(y-\frac{19}{36}\right)^2 =\frac{545}{648}
	\]
	This is an arc of a Euclidean circle centered at $C=\left(\frac 54,\frac{19}{36}\right)$ with radius $r=\sqrt{\frac{545}{648}}$. 
\end{example}


\paragraph{Distance and Angle in the Poincaré  Disk}

The undefined terms \emph{point, line, on} and \emph{between} now make sense. To complete the model, we need to define \emph{congruence} of segments and angles.

\begin{defn}{}{poincaredisk2}
	The \emph{hyperbolic distance} between points $P,Q$ in the Poincaré disk is\footnotemark{}\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
		\[
			d(P,Q):=\cosh^{-1}\left(1+\frac{2\nm{PQ}^2}{(1-\nm{P}^2)(1-\nm{Q}^2)}\right)
		\]
		where $\nm{PQ}$, $\nm P=\nm{OP}$ and $\nm Q=\nm{OQ}$ are Euclidean distances.\par
		Hyperbolic segments are \emph{congruent} if they have the same length
		\[
			\cl{AB}\cong\cl{PQ}\iff d(A,B)=d(P,Q)
		\]
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={angles are the same as in euclidean geometry}]{models-angle}
	\end{minipage}\smallbreak
	The \emph{angle} between hyperbolic rays is that between their Euclidean tangent rays. Angles are \emph{congruent} if they are so in a Euclidean sense.\smallbreak
	The \emph{hyperbolic circle} with center $P$ and (hyperbolic) radius $\rho$ is the set of points $X$ for which $d(X,P)=\rho$. 
\end{defn}

\footnotetext{\label{fn:invcosh}%
	It should seem reasonable for hyperbolic functions to play some role in hyperbolic geometry! For reference:
  \[
  	\cosh x=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2,\quad \sinh x=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2,\quad \cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1,\quad\cosh^{-1}x=\ln(x+\sqrt{x^2-1})
  \]
}

It is worth observing both the symmetry of the distance function ($d(P,Q)=d(Q,P)$) and how it simplifies when measuring from the origin: if $Q=O$, then
\[
	d(O,P)=\cosh^{-1}\frac{1+\nm P^2}{1-\nm P^2}=\ln\frac{1+\nm P}{1-\nm P}
\]



\begin{example}{}{hypisosceles}
	We calculate the side-lengths and angles in the hyperbolic isosceles right-triangle with vertices $O=(0,0)$, $P=(\frac 12,0)$ and $Q=(0,\frac 12)$.\smallbreak
	To compute the hyperbolic distances, we first need the three \emph{Euclidean} versions:\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.69\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt} 
		\begin{gather*}
		\nm P=\frac 12=\nm Q,\qquad \nm{PQ}^2=\frac 14+\frac 14=\frac 12\\
			d(O,P) =d(O,Q) =\ln\frac{1+\frac 12}{1-\frac 12}
			=\ln 3 =\cosh^{-1}\frac 53\approx 1.10\\
			d(P,Q) =\cosh^{-1}\left(1+\frac{2\cdot \frac 12}{(1-\frac 14)^2}\right) 
			=\cosh^{-1}\frac{25}9 \approx 1.68
		\end{gather*}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.3\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={measuring the sides of a hyperbolic triangle}]{models-dist4}
	\end{minipage}\par
	To find the interior angle $\theta$, implicitly differentiate the equation for the hyperbolic line $\lin{PQ}$:
	\[
		x^2+y^2-\frac 52x-\frac 52y+1=0 \implies \diffat[y]{x}{P}=\frac{4x-5}{5-4y}\bigg|_P=-\frac 35 \implies \theta=\tan^{-1}\frac 35\approx \ang{31}
	\]
	By symmetry, we have the same angle at $Q$. With a right-angle at $O$, we conclude that the angle-sum is $\Sigma_{\triangle}\approx\ang{152}$! To satisfy curiosity, we compare data for $\triangle OPQ$ and the \emph{Euclidean} triangle with the same vertices
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{l||c|c}
			Property&Hyperbolic Triangle&Euclidean Triangle\\\hline\hline
			Edge lengths&$1.10:1.10:1.68$&$0.5:0.5:0.71$\\
			Relative edge ratios&$1:1:1.53$&$1:1:1.41$\\
			Angles&\ang{31}, \ang{31}, \ang{90}&\ang{45}, \ang{45}, \ang{90}%\\
			%Area&1.023&0.322
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	The hyperbolic triangle has longer sides and a \emph{relatively} longer hypotenuse. Moreover, its side lengths do \emph{not} satisfy the Pythagorean relation\footnotemark{} $a^2+b^2=c^2$.
\end{example}

\footnotetext{Though the relationship $\cosh a\cosh b=\cosh c$ should be obvious. We'll return to this observation later\ldots}


The Poincaré disk uses concepts from \emph{Euclidean} (analytic) geometry to define objects and compute in \emph{hyperbolic} geometry. It may seem laborious to keep stressing the distinction, but it is essential not to confuse the corresponding versions of distance, line, circle, etc. While the meaning of \emph{angle} is unchanged, everything else is at least slightly, if not significantly, different.\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.73\linewidth}\vspace{-5pt}

Observe how the centers are closer (in a Euclidean sense) to the boundary circle (omega-points) than one might expect; this is since hyperbolic distance measure is greater the further one is from the origin.\smallbreak
	In fact (Exercise \ref{exs:circhypcirc}) hyperbolic circles in the Poincaré disk model are also Euclidean circles! Their hyperbolic \textcolor{mGreen}{radii} moreover meet the circles at right-angles, as we'd expect.
\end{minipage}

\paragraph{A Little Bookkeeping}

The next result is an exercise; it says that the distance function behaves roughly as we expect, increasing smoothly as one moves along a hyperbolic line.
	
\begin{lemm}{}{hypdistwd}
	Fix $P$ and a hyperbolic line $\ell$ through $P$. Then the distance function $Q\mapsto d(P,Q)$ maps the points of $\ell$ on one side of $P$ differentiably and bijectively onto the interval $(0,\infty)$.
\end{lemm}
	
This should also convince us that hyperbolic circles look roughly as we expect. Of greater importance is the following.

\begin{thm}{}{}
	The Poincaré disk is a model of hyperbolic geometry.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}[Sketch Proof]
	A rigorous proof would require us to check the hyperbolic postulate and all Hilbert's axioms except Playfair. Instead we verify Euclid's postulates 1--4 and the hyperbolic postulate 5.
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item Lemma \ref{lemm:hypline} says we can join any given points in the Poincaré disk by a unique segment.
	  \item A hyperbolic segment joins two points \emph{inside} the (open) Poincaré disk. The distance formula increases (Lemma \ref{lemm:hypdistwd}) unboundedly as $P$ moves towards the boundary circle, so we can always make a hyperbolic segment longer.
	  \item Hyperbolic circles are as in Definition \ref{defn:poincaredisk2}.
	  \item All right-angles are equal since the notion of angle is unchanged from Euclidean geometry.
	  \item The first picture on page \pageref{sec:hyp-models} shows multiple parallels!\hfill{\qedhere}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}





\subsubsection*{Other Models of Hyperbolic Space (non-examinable)}

There are several other models of hyperbolic space. Here are three of the most common.

\paragraph{Klein Disk Model}

This is similar to the Poincaré disk. Instead of Euclidean arcs, hyperbolic lines are chords of the unit circle (`Euclidean' line-segments) without their end-points, and the distance function is different: if  $\Omega,\Theta$ are the omega-points of the chord $\smash[t]{\lin{PQ}}$, then\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.67\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
	\[

\begin{thm}{Fundamental Theorem of Parallels in Hyperbolic Geometry}{hyperfund}\phantom{bob}\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.73\linewidth}\vspace{-10pt}
		Given $P\not\in\mathcolor{dRed}{\ell}$, drop the perpendicular $\cl{PQ}$ to $\ell$. Then there exist precisely two \textbf{limiting,} or \textbf{asymptotic, parallels} $\mathcolor{Blue}{m,n}$ to $\ell$ through $P$ with the following properties:
		\begin{enumerate}
	  	\item A \textcolor{dMagenta}{ray} based at $P$ intersects $\ell$ if and only if it lies between $m$ and $n$ in the same fashion as $\ray{PQ}$.
	  	\item The limiting parallels make congruent acute angles $\mu$ with $\ray{PQ}$, the \textbf{angle of parallelism} at $P$ relative to $\ell$.
		\end{enumerate}		
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-10pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={limiting parallels from a point not on a given line}]{basic-parallels}
	\end{minipage}
\end{thm}

Every other (non-asymptotic) parallel is called an \emph{ultraparallel.} More generally, parallel lines $\ell,m$ are \emph{asymptotic} if they `meet' at an omega-point.\smallbreak


The proof depends crucially on ideas from analysis, particularly continuity \& suprema. As you read through, consider how everything \emph{except} the last line is valid in Euclidean geometry!
 
\begin{proof}
	Points $R\in\ell$ are in continuous bijective correspondence with the real numbers (Lemma \ref{lemm:hypdistwd}). We therefore have a \emph{continuous, increasing} function
	\[
		f:\R\to(-\ang{90},\ang{90})\quad\text{where}\quad f(r)=\measuredangle QPR
		\tag{$\pm\ang{90}\not\in\operatorname{range} f$ by Saccheri--Legendre}
	\]
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.73\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		Since $\dom f=\R$ is an interval, the intermediate value theorem says that $\operatorname{range}f$ is a \emph{subinterval} $I\subseteq(-\ang{90},\ang{90})$.\smallbreak
		Given $R\in\ell$, transfer $\cl{QR}$ to the other side of $Q$ to obtain $S\in\ell$. By SAS, $\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\measuredangle QPS}=\mathcolor{mMagenta}{-\measuredangle QPR}$ whence $I$ is \emph{symmetric}: $\theta\in I\Longleftrightarrow -\theta\in I$.\smallbreak
	Let $\mu:=\sup I\in(\ang{0},\ang{90}]$ be the least upper bound; by symmetry, $\inf I=-\mu$. Let $m$ and $n$ be the lines making angles $\pm\mu$ respectively. Certainly any ray between $m,n$ intersects $\ell$. To finish part 1, it remains only to see that $m,n$ are parallel to $\ell$ (that is, $I=(-\mu,\mu)$).
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-15pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={any ray between the limits must intersect l}]{basic-parallels5}
	\end{minipage}\smallbreak
	For contradiction, suppose $m$ intersects $\ell$ at $M$. Transfer $\cl{QM}$ to the other side of $M$ to obtain $\tilde M$. Since $f$ is increasing, $\measuredangle QP\tilde M>\mu=\measuredangle QPM$ contradicts $\mu=\sup I$.\smallbreak
	Finally $m=n\Longleftrightarrow \mu=\ang{90}$. In such a case there would exist only one parallel to $\ell$ through $P$, contradicting the hyperbolic postulate.
\end{proof}


\pagebreak[3]


The picture suggests a bijective relationship between $\mu$ and the perpendicular distance. Here it is; a simplified argument is in Exercise \ref*{sec:hypparallel}.\ref{exs:angparallelism}, and the full result to the next section.

\begin{cor}{}{parangleformula}
	The perpendicular distance $\delta=d(P,Q)$ and the angle of parallelism are related via
	\[
		\cosh\delta=\csc\mu
		\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad 
		\smash{\tan\frac{\mu}2}=e^{-\delta}
	\]
\end{cor}

\subsubsection*{Angles in Triangles, Rectangles and the AAA Congruence}

We at last come to the triple punchline. Everything follows from one big picture.

\begin{thm}{}{absgeomnorect}
	Suppose $\square ABCD$ is a rectangle in absolute geometry. Then the angle of parallelism of $B$ with respect to $\ell:=\lin{AD}$ is \ang{90}. Otherwise said, $\lin{BC}$ is the unique parallel to $\lin{AD}$ through $B$.
\end{thm}


\begin{proof}
	Reflect $\square ABCD$ across $\smash[t]{\cl{CD}}$ and repeat to obtain an infinite family of congruent rectangles (Exercise \ref*{sec:hyp1}.\ref{exs:saccherisplit}). Let $\smash[t]{P\in\cl{CD}}$ and drop perpendiculars to $\smash[t]{R\in\cl{AB}}$ and $C_1$ as shown.
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[alt={a rectangle in absolute geometry}]{basic-rect4}\vspace{-4pt}
	\end{center}
	We claim $\square PRBC$ is a rectangle: if not, then one of $\square ARPD$ or $\square PRBC$ would have angle-sum exceeding \ang{360} and contradicting Saccheri--Legendre. Similarly $\square DPC_1D_1$ is a rectangle.\smallbreak
	By Exercise \ref*{sec:hyp1}.\ref{exs:rectanglesplit}, $\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\ray{BP}}$ splits $\square PRBC$ into a pair of congruent triangles. In particular, $\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\ray{BP}}$ crosses $\cl{CD}$ at the \textcolor{Brown}{same angle} it leaves $B$.\smallbreak
	Iterate to obtain the picture: the small rectangles are congruent (essentially Exercise \ref*{sec:hyp1}.\ref{exs:saccherisplit} again), whence $P,Q_1,Q_2,\ldots$ is an \emph{equidistant} sequence along $\ray{CD}$.\smallbreak
	Since $\cl{CD}$ is \emph{finite,} the Archimedean property (Exercise \ref*{sec:circ}.\ref{exs:archimedes}) says that this sequence eventually passes $D$: some $Q_n$ lies on the opposite side of $\ell$ to $B$, whence $P_n\in\mathcolor{Magenta}{\ray{BP}}$ does also. We conclude that $\textcolor{Magenta}{\ray{BP}}$ intersects $\ell$.\smallbreak
	Since $P\in\cl{CD}$ was generic, the angle of parallelism of $B$ with respect to $\ell$ is \ang{90}.
\end{proof}


\begin{cor}{}{hyptriangle}
	Within hyperbolic geometry:
	\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
	  \item There are \textbf{no rectangles} (quadrilaterals with four right-angles). In particular, the summit angles of a Saccheri quadrilateral are \textbf{acute} (compare Theorem \ref{thm:summit90}).
	  \item The angles in a triangle sum to \textbf{strictly less} than \ang{180}.
	  \item (AAA congruence)\lstsp If the angles of $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle DEF$ are congruent in pairs, then the triangles are \textbf{congruent} ($\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF$).
	\end{enumerate}
\end{cor}

Part 1 is simply the Theorem: a unique parallel contradicts the hyperbolic postulate. We leave the rest to Exercises \ref{exs:triless180} and \ref{exs:aaacongproof}. Notice that AAA is a \emph{congruence} theorem in hyperbolic geometry, not a \emph{similarity} theorem (compare Theorem \ref{thm:aaasim}). Finally, revisit the observations on page \pageref{pg:absolute}; Euclid's arguments making use of the parallel postulate genuinely \emph{require} it!

\subsection{Omega-triangles}\label{sec:omegatri}

Recall that limiting parallels (Theorem \ref{thm:hyperfund}) `meet' at an omega-point.\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.72\linewidth}\vspace{-2pt}
	\begin{defn}{}{}
		An \emph{omega-triangle} or \emph{ideal-triangle} is a `triangle' where at least two sides are limiting parallels. Alternatively (in the Poincaré disk model), one or more of the vertices is an omega-point.
	\end{defn}
	
	The three types of omega-triangle depend on how many omega-points they have. In the picture, $\triangle PQ\Omega$ has one omega-point, $\triangle P\Omega\Theta$ has two, and $\triangle \Omega\Theta\Xi$ three!
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-10pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={examples of omega triangles}]{omega-example}
\end{minipage}\smallbreak
Amazingly, many of the standard results of absolute geometry also apply to omega-triangles! The first can be thought of as the AAA congruence theorem where one `angle' is zero.

\begin{thm}{Angle-Angle Congruence for Omega-triangles}{}
	Suppose $\triangle AB\Omega$ and $\triangle PQ\Theta$ are omega-triangles, each with a single omega-point. If the (finite) angles are congruent in pairs
	\[
		\mathcolor{dMagenta}{\angle AB\Omega} \cong \mathcolor{dMagenta}{\angle PQ\Theta} \qquad \angle BA\Omega \cong\angle QP\Theta
	\]
	then the finite sides of the omega-triangles are also congruent: $\cl{AB}\cong\cl{PQ}$.
\end{thm}

Remember that omega-points are not really part of hyperbolic geometry---their appearance in our description is a quirk of the Poincaré disk model. It therefore doesn't make much sense to speak of congruent infinite sides or of congruent angles at omega-points.

\begin{proof}
	WLOG and for contradiction, assume $\cl{AB}>\cl{PQ}$. Transfer $\mathcolor{dRed}{\angle QP\Theta}$ to $A$ to obtain $C\in\cl{AB}$ such that $\cl{AC}\cong\cl{PQ}$. Transferring $\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle PQ\Theta}$ creates a ray $\mathcolor{mGreen}{r}$ based at $C$ on the same side as $\Omega$. Exercise \ref{exs:aacongproof} verifies $\mathcolor{mGreen}{r=\ray{C\Omega}}$. Our hypothesis is that the pictured \textcolor{mMagenta}{angles} at $B$ and $C$ are congruent.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.73\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
% 	If $r$ intersected $\ray{A\Omega}$ we have a (finite) triangle which would force (ASA) $\triangle AB\Omega$ to be finite. If $r$ intersected $\ray{B\Omega}$ we'd have a triangle contradicting the Exterior Angle Theorem. 
		Let $M$ be the midpoint of $\mathcolor{Blue}{\smash{\cl{BC}}}$ and drop the perpendicular to $\smash{R\in\lin{B\Omega}}$.\smallbreak
		Let $\smash{S\in\lin{C\Omega}}$ lie on the opposite side of $\smash{\overleftrightarrow{BC}}$ to $R$ such that $\mathcolor{Brown}{\smash{\cl{CS}}}\cong \mathcolor{Brown}{\smash{\cl{BR}}}$.\smallbreak
		By \textcolor{Blue}{Side}-\textcolor{mMagenta}{Angle}-\textcolor{Brown}{Side} we have $\triangle MBR\cong\triangle MCS$. In particular:
		\begin{itemize}
		  \item $\triangle MCS$ is right-angled(!) at $S$.
		  \item Congruent vertical angles at $M$ force $M$ to lie on the segment $\cl{RS}$.
		\end{itemize}
		The angle of parallelism of $S$ relative to $\lin{B\Omega}$ is therefore $\angle RS\Omega=\ang{90}$, contradicting the Fundamental Theorem (\ref{thm:hyperfund}).
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-4pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={proving the A A congruence theorem for omega triangles}]{basic-ext5}
	\end{minipage}\smallbreak
	There are two other possible orientations:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $R$ could lie on the opposite side of $B$ from $\Omega$. In this case SAS is applied to the same triangles but with respect to congruent \textcolor{mMagenta}{supplementary angles}.
	  \item In the special case $R=B$, the same contradiction appears: the angle of parallelism of $C$ with respect to $\smash{\lin{B\Omega}}$ is \ang{90}.\qedhere
	\end{itemize}
\end{proof}




\pagebreak[3]


\begin{thm}{Exterior Angle Theorem for Omega-Triangles}{exthyp}
	Suppose $\triangle BC\Omega$ has a single omega-point and that $C$ lies between $A$ and $B$. Then $\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle AC\Omega}>\mathcolor{dMagenta}{\angle CB\Omega}$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	We show that the two other cases are impossible.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.73\linewidth}\vspace{-3pt}
		\begin{description}\itemsep1pt
		  \item[\normalfont ($\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle AC\Omega}\cong\mathcolor{dMagenta}{\angle CB\Omega}$)] This is precisely the contradictory arrangement described in the previous proof!
			\item[\normalfont ($\mathcolor{Brown}{\angle AC\Omega}<\mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle CB\Omega}$)] Transfer the \textcolor{mMagenta}{latter} to $C$ to produce $\smash{\ray{CX}}$ \emph{interior} to $\angle BC\Omega$ with $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\angle ACX}\cong \mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle CB\Omega}$.\par
			Since $\smash{\lin{C\Omega}}$ is a limiting parallel to $\smash{\lin{C\Omega}}$, the Fundamental Theorem says that $\smash{\ray{CX}}$ intersects $\smash{\lin{B\Omega}}$ at some point $Y$.\par
			But now $\triangle CBY$ contradicts the standard exterior angle theorem ($\mathcolor{mGreen}{\angle ACY}\cong \mathcolor{mMagenta}{\angle CBY}$).
		\end{description}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-23pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={the exterior angle theorem for omega triangles}]{basic-ext4}
	\end{minipage}\vspace{-5pt}
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}{Side-Angle Congruence for Omega-triangles}{}
	Suppose $\triangle BC\Omega$ and $\triangle PQ\Theta$ both have a single omega-point. If $\mathcolor{Magenta}{\angle CB\Omega} \cong\angle QP\Theta$ and $\mathcolor{blue}{\cl{BC}}\cong\cl{PQ}$ then $\angle BC\Omega \cong\angle PQ\Theta$.
\end{cor}

The final congruence theorem is an exercise based on the previous picture.
In summary: A triangle with a single omega-point has three pieces of data (two finite angles and one edge). The AA and SA congruence theorems say that two of these determine the third.

\subsection{Area and Angle-defect}\label{sec:hyparea}

In this section we consider one of the triumphs of Johann Lambert: the relationship between the sum of the angles in a triangle and its \emph{area.} We start with a loose axiomatization of area as a relative measure. Until explicitly stated otherwise, we work in \emph{absolute geometry.}

\begin{description}
	\item[Axiom I] Two geometric figures have the same area if and only if they may be sub-divided into finitely many pairs of mutually congruent triangles.\footnote{%
	To allow infinitely many infinitesimal sub-triangles would require ideas from analysis\ldots%
	}
	\item[Axiom II] The area of a triangle is positive.
	\item[Axiom III] The area of a union of disjoint figures is the sum of the areas of the figures.
\end{description}

\begin{defn}{Angle defect}{}
	Let $\Sigma_{\triangle}$ be the sum of the angles in a triangle. Measured in radians, the \emph{angle-defect} of $\triangle$ is $\pi-\Sigma_{\triangle}$.
\end{defn}

Since triangles in absolute geometry have $\Sigma_{\triangle}\le\pi$ (Theorem \ref{thm:saccleg}), it follows that
\[
	0\le\pi-\Sigma_{\triangle}\le\pi
\]
In Euclidean geometry the defect is always zero; in hyperbolic geometry it is strictly positive (Corollary \ref{cor:hyptriangle}). A `triangle' with three omega-points would have defect $\pi$.

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.74\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	\begin{lemm}{}{}
		Angle-defect is additive: If a triangle is split into two sub-triangles, then the defect of the whole is the sum of the defects of the parts.
	\end{lemm}
	
	This is immediate from the picture:
	\[
		\left[\pi-(\alpha+\gamma+\epsilon)\right]+\left[\pi-(\beta+\delta+\zeta)\right]=\pi-(\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta)
	\]
	since $\epsilon+\zeta=\pi$. Notice that angle-\emph{sum} is not additive!
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={comparing the angle sums in sub triangles}]{area-defect}
\end{minipage}



\begin{thm}{Area determines angle-sum in \emph{absolute geometry}}{areatoanglesum}
	If triangles have the same area, then their angle-sums are identical.
\end{thm}

Of course this trivial in Euclidean geometry where all triangles have the same angle-sum!

\begin{proof}
	The lemma provides the induction step: if $\triangle_1$ and $\triangle_2$ have the same area, then their interiors are disjoint unions of a finite collection of mutually congruent triangles:
	\[
		\triangle_1=\bigcup_{k=1}^n\triangle_{1,k}\quad\text{and}\quad \triangle_2=\bigcup_{k=1}^n\triangle_{2,k}\quad\text{where}\quad \triangle_{1,k}\cong\triangle_{2,k}
	\]
	Each sub-pair $\triangle_{1,k},\triangle_{2,k}$ has the same defect, whence the defects of $\triangle_1$ and $\triangle_2$ are equal:
	\[
		\operatorname{defect}(\triangle_1)=\sum_{k=1}^n\operatorname{defect}(\triangle_{1,k}) =\sum_{k=1}^n\operatorname{defect}(\triangle_{2,k}) =\operatorname{defect}(\triangle_2)\tag*{\qedhere}
	\]
\end{proof}

\pagebreak[3]


\subsubsection*{Angle-sum determines area in \emph{hyperbolic geometry}}

The converse in hyperbolic geometry relies on a beautiful construction generalizing that of Theorem \ref{thm:summit90}. The construction is valid in absolute geometry, though the ultimate conclusion that angle-sum determines area is not.% If the initial discussion seems difficult, pretend you are in Euclidean geometry and think about \emph{rectangles.}

\begin{lemm}{}{quadtricorr}
	The picture below illustrates the result of two constructions (2 is the reverse of 1):
	\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
	  \item Given $\triangle ABC$, bisect sides $\cl{AB}$ and $\cl{AC}$ at $E,F$ respectively and drop \textcolor{dRed}{perpendiculars} from the vertices to $\lin{EF}$. Then $\square HICB$ is a Saccheri quadrilateral with summit $\cl{BC}$.
	  \item Given a Saccheri quadrilateral $\square HICB$ with summit $\cl{BC}$, let $A$ be any point such that $\lin{HI}$ bisects $\cl{AB}$ at $E$. Then the intersection $F=\lin{HI}\cap\cl{AC}$ is the midpoint of $\cl{AC}$.
	\end{enumerate}
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
	Regardless of direction, we have the following conclusions:
	  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
	    \item The triangle and quadrilateral have equal area.
	    \item The sum of the summit angles of the quadrilateral equals the angle-sum of the triangle.
	  \end{itemize}
	  We chose $\cl{BC}$ to be the longest side of $\triangle ABC$---this isn't necessary, though it helpfully forces $E,F$ to lie between $H,I$.
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.39\linewidth}\vspace{-18pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={Comparing area and angle-sum for triangles and quadrilaterals}]{area-saccheri4}
	\end{minipage}
\end{lemm}


\begin{proof}
	Part 1 is simply two applications of the SAA congruence theorem (follow the arrows!)
	\[
	  \triangle BEH\cong\triangle AEG\text{ \ and \ }\triangle CFI\cong\triangle AFG
	\]
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
		The area and angle-sum correspondence is immediate.\medbreak
		For part 2, suppose the midpoint of $\cl{AC}$ were at $J\neq F$. By part 1, we may create a new Saccheri quadrilateral with summit $\cl{BC}$ using the midpoints $E,J$.\smallbreak
		By Lemma \ref{lemm:saccheribasic}, the perpendicular bisector of $\cl{BC}$ bisects the bases of both Saccheri quadrilaterals, creating $\triangle EUV$ with two right-angles: contradiction.
 	\end{minipage}
 	\hfill
 	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.39\linewidth}\vspace{-25pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={proving that the construction is reversible}]{area-saccheri5}
 	\end{minipage}	
\end{proof}

We now prove a special case of the main result.

\begin{lemm}{}{anglesumarea}
	Suppose \textbf{hyperbolic} triangles $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle PQR$ have congruent sides $\cl{BC}\cong\cl{QR}$ and the same angle-sum. Then the triangles have the same area.
\end{lemm}


\begin{proof}
	Construct the quadrilaterals corresponding to $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle PQR$ with summits $\cl{BC}\cong\cl{QR}$. These have congruent summits \emph{and} summit angles: by Exercise \ref*{sec:hypparallel}.\ref{exs:saccherisummitcong} they are congruent.
\end{proof}

The final observation is what makes this special to \emph{hyperbolic} geometry. In the Euclidean case, Saccheri quadrilaterals are \emph{rectangles}, and congruent summits do not force congruence of the remaining sides.



\begin{thm}{}{arealemm2}
	In \textbf{hyperbolic} geometry, if $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle PQR$ have the same angle-sum then they have the same area.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
	If the triangles have a pair of congruent edges, the previous result says we are done. Otherwise, use Lemma \ref{lemm:quadtricorr} to create a new triangle $\triangle LBC$ which matches the same Saccheri quadrilateral as $\triangle ABC$.\medbreak
	WLOG suppose $\nm{AB}<\nm{PQ}$ and construct the Saccheri quadrilateral with summit $\cl{BC}$. Select $K$ on $\lin{EF}$ such that $\nm{BK}=\frac 12\nm{PQ}$ and extend such that $K$ is the midpoint of $\cl{BL}$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\begin{itemize}
		  \item By Lemma \ref{lemm:quadtricorr},
		  \[\operatorname{Area}(\triangle LBC)=\operatorname{Area}(HICB)=\operatorname{Area}(\triangle ABC)\]
		  \item By Theorem \ref{thm:areatoanglesum}, $\triangle LBC$ has the same angle-sum as $\triangle ABC$ and thus $\triangle PQR$.
		  \item $\triangle LBC$ and $\triangle PQR$ share a congruent side ($\cl{LB}\cong\cl{PQ}$) and have the same angle-sum. Lemma \ref{lemm:anglesumarea} says their areas are equal.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.39\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={angle sums determine area in hyperbolic geometry}]{area-saccheri9}\qedhere
	\end{minipage}
\end{proof}

Since both area and angle-\emph{defect} are additive, we immediately conclude:

\begin{cor}{}{hypareaangle}
	The angle-defect of a hyperbolic triangle is an additive function of its area. By normalizing the definition of area,\footnotemark{} we may conclude that
	\[
		\pi-\Sigma_{\triangle}=\operatorname{Area}\triangle
	\]
\end{cor}

\footnotetext{
	We have really only proved that $\pi-\Sigma_{\triangle}$ is proportional to $\operatorname{Area}\triangle$. However, it can be seen that these quantities are equal if we use the area measure arising naturally from the hyperbolic distance function (Definition \ref{defn:poincaredisk2}).\smallbreak
 	Corollary \ref{cor:hypareaangle} is a special case of the famous Gauss--Bonnet theorem from differential geometry: for any triangle on a surface with Gauss curvature $K$, we have
	\[
		\Sigma_{\triangle}-\pi=\iint_{\triangle} K\,\dA
	\]
	We've now met all three special constant-curvature examples of this:
	\begin{description}
		\item[Euclidean space] is \emph{flat} ($K=0$) so the angle-defect is always zero.
		\item[Hyperbolic space] has \emph{constant negative curvature} $K=-1$, whence $\iint_{\triangle}\dA=-(\Sigma_{\triangle}-\pi)$ is the angle-defect.
		\item[Spherical geometry] A radius 1 sphere has \emph{constant positive curvature} $K=1$ and $\iint_{\triangle}\dA$ is the angle-\emph{excess} $\Sigma_{\triangle}-\pi$. 
	\end{description}
}

Note finally how the AAA congruence (Corollary \ref{cor:hyptriangle}, part 3) is related to the corollary:
\[
	\begin{array}{ccc}
		\triangle ABC\cong \triangle DEF & \overset{\text{AAA}}{\iff} & \text{angles congruent in pairs}\\[5pt]
		\Downarrow & & \Downarrow\\
		\text{equal area} & \overset{\text{Cor \ref{cor:hypareaangle}}}{\iff} & \text{same angle-defect}
	\end{array}
\]


\begin{example*}{\ref{ex:hypisosceles}, cont}{}
	The isosceles right-triangle with vertices $O$, $P=(\frac 12,0)$ and $Q=(0,\frac 12)$ has angle-sum and area
	\[
		\frac\pi 2+2\tan^{-1}\frac 35\approx \ang{151.93}\implies \operatorname{area}=\pi-\left(\frac\pi 2+2\tan^{-1}\frac 35\right) =\frac\pi 2-2\tan^{-1}\frac 35\approx 0.490
	\]
	A Euclidean triangle with the same vertices has much smaller area: $\frac 12\cdot\frac 12\cdot\frac 12=\frac 18=0.125$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.73\linewidth}\vspace{-2pt}
		Generalizing this (Exercise \ref*{sec:hyp-models}.\ref{exs:isorighthypextended}), the triangle with vertices $O,P=(c,0)$ and $Q=(0,c)$ has area
		\[
			\pi-\left(\frac\pi 2+2\tan^{-1}\frac{1-c^2}{1+c^2}\right)=\frac\pi 2-2\tan^{-1}\frac{1-c^2}{1+c^2}
		\]
		As expected, $\lim\limits_{c\to 0^+}\text{area}(c)=0$. In the other limit, the triangle becomes an omega-triangle with two omega-points and $\lim\limits_{c\to 1^-}\text{area}(c)=\frac\pi 2$: an infinite `triangle' with finite `area'!
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\hfill
		\begin{tabular}{c@{}}
			\includegraphics[alt={an omega triangle with area pi on two}]{area-iso}\\
			The limit $c\to 1^-$
		\end{tabular}
	\end{minipage}
\end{example*}

Our discussion in fact provides an explicit method for cutting a triangle into sub-triangles and rearranging its pieces to create a triangle with equal area.

\begin{center}
	\includegraphics[alt={transforming a triangle into another with equal area}]{area-saccheri10}
	\qquad\qquad
	\includegraphics[alt={transforming a triangle into another with equal area}]{area-saccheri8}
\end{center}

Suppose $\mathcolor{mRed}{\triangle_1}$ and $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle_2}$ have equal areas and construct the quadrilaterals $\mathcolor{mRed}{\square_1}$ and $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\square_2}$. Let $L,K$ be chosen so that $\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{BL}\cong\cl{QR}}$ and $K$ is the midpoint of $\mathcolor{Blue}{\cl{BL}}$. We now have:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathcolor{mRed}{\triangle_1}, \mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle_2}, \mathcolor{Blue}{\triangle_3}, \mathcolor{mRed}{\square_1}, \mathcolor{mGreen}{\square_2}, \mathcolor{Blue}{\square_3}$ have the same area.
  \item The summit angles of $\mathcolor{mRed}{\square_1}, \mathcolor{mGreen}{\square_2}, \mathcolor{Blue}{\square_3}$ are congruent (half the angle-sum of each triangle).
  \item $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\square_2}, \mathcolor{Blue}{\square_3}$ are \emph{congruent} since they have congruent summits and summit angles.
\end{itemize}

We can now follow the steps in Lemma \ref{lemm:quadtricorr} to transform $\mathcolor{mRed}{\triangle_1}$ into $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle_2}$:
\[
	\mathcolor{mRed}{\triangle_1}\to \mathcolor{mRed}{\square_1}\to \mathcolor{Blue}{\triangle_3}\to \mathcolor{Blue}{\square_3}\cong \mathcolor{mGreen}{\square_2}\to \mathcolor{mGreen}{\triangle_2}
\]
where each arrow represents cutting off two sub-triangles and moving them. Indeed this works even for triangles in Euclidean geometry!

	  \begin{enumerate}
	    \item Prove that the hyperbolic segment $\cl{P\Omega}$ is an arc of a circle with equation
	    \[
	    	(x-1)^2+(y-k)^2=k^2 \tag{some $k>0$}
	    \]
	    \item Prove that the area of $\triangle OP\Omega$ is given by
			\[
				A(h)=\sin^{-1}\dfrac{2h}{1+h^2}
			\]
		\end{enumerate}
		
		
		\item Suppose two Saccheri quadrilaterals in absolute geometry have the same area and congruent summits. Prove that the quadrilaterals are congruent.
	
	\end{enumerate}
\end{exercises}

\subsection{Isometries and Calculation}\label{sec:hyperiso}

There are (at least!) two major issues in our approach to hyperbolic geometry.

\begin{description}
	\item[Calculations are difficult] In (Euclidean) analytic geometry we typically choose the origin and orient axes to ease calculation. We'd like to do the same in hyperbolic geometry.
	\item[Too many assumptions] \emph{Distance, angle} and \emph{line} were defined separately, but in (Euclidean) analytic geometry, these concepts are \emph{not independent}: the distance function defines the other two.\footnote{%
		The Pythagorean distance function $\nm{\vu}=\nm{PQ}=\sqrt{(q_x-p_x)^2+(q_y-p_y)^2)}$ defines the dot product and therefore the angle measure,
		\[
			\nm\vu\nm\vv\cos\theta=\vu\cdot\vv=\frac 12\left(\nm{\vu+\vv}^2-\nm\vu^2-\nm\vv^2\right)
		\]
		With some calculus, the arc-length $\int_C\ds=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\nm{\vr'(t)}\dt$ of any parametrized curve may be computed. One then shows that the paths of shortest length (\emph{geodesics}) are straight lines.
	}%
\end{description}

Isometries provide a related remedy for these issues. To describe these it is helpful to start with an alternative definition of the Poincaré disk and its distance function.

\begin{defn}{}{hypdist2}
	The \emph{Poincaré disk} is the set $D:=\{z\in\C:\nm z<1\}$ equipped with the distance function\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.7\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
		\[
			d(z,w):=\nm{\ln\frac{\nm{z-\Omega}\nm{w-\Theta}}{\nm{z-\Theta}\nm{w-\Omega}}}
		\]
		where $\Omega,\Theta$ are the omega-points for the hyperbolic line through $z,w$ (a diameter, or a circular arc intersecting the boundary circle perpendicularly).
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.29\linewidth}\vspace{-18pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={omega points at the ends of a hyperbolic line}]{isom-dist}
	\end{minipage}
\end{defn}

It is not worth memorizing this formula: Corollary \ref{cor:hypcossinerule} will show that this is exactly the same as the original cosh formula (Definition \ref{defn:poincaredisk2}). Indeed it is already easy to check that we get the same formula when $z\neq 0=w$: since the hyperbolic line $\lin{zw}$ is the diameter of $D$ through $z$, its omega-points are $\Omega,\Theta=\pm\frac z{\nm z}$, and we easily compute the distance
\[
	d(z,0)=\nm{\ln\frac{\nm{z-\frac z{\nm z}}\nm{0+\frac z{\nm z}}}{\nm{z+\frac z{\nm z}}\nm{0-\frac z{\nm z}}}}
	=\ln\frac{1+\nm z}{1-\nm z}
\]
exactly as before. Showing that this distance function also determines the usual angle-measure and that paths of shortest distance are indeed hyperbolic lines requires some multivariable calculus.\footnote{%
	A non-examinable discussion of this begins on page \pageref{pg:hyp1stform}.
}

\subsubsection*{A Little Complex Analysis}

For candidate isometries we need functions $f:D\to D$ for which $d\bigl(f(z),f(w)\bigr)=d(z,w)$. These follow from some standard results of complex analysis, which we state without proof.


\begin{thm}{Möbius/fractional-linear transformations}{mobius}
	If $a,b,c,d\in\C$ and $ad-bc\neq 0$, then the function $f(z)=\tfrac{az+b}{cz+d}$ has the following properties:
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item (Invertibility)\lstsp $f:\C\cup\{\infty\}\to \C\cup\{\infty\}$ is bijective, with inverse $f^{-1}(z)=\tfrac{dz-b}{-cz+a}$.
	  \item (Conformality)\lstsp If curves intersect, then their images under $f$ intersect at the same angle.
	  \item (Line/circle preservation)\lstsp Every line/circle\footnotemark{} in $\C$ is mapped by $f$ to another line/circle.
	  \item(Cross-ratio preservation)\lstsp Given distinct $z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4$, we have
		\[
			\frac{\big(f(z_1)-f(z_2)\big)\big(f(z_3)-f(z_4)\big)}{\big(f(z_2)-f(z_3)\big)\big(f(z_4)-f(z_1)\big)} =\frac{(z_1-z_2)(z_3-z_4)}{(z_2-z_3)(z_4-z_1)}
		\]\vspace{-5pt}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\footnotetext{%
	In $\C\cup\{\infty\}$ a line is just a circle containing $\infty$!%
}


The isometries of the Poincaré disk are a subset of the Möbius transformations.

\begin{thm}{}{hypisomclass}
	The \textbf{orientation-preserving\footnotemark{} isometries} of the Poincaré disk have the form
	\[
		f(z)=e^{i\theta}\frac{\alpha-z}{\cl\alpha z-1}
		\quad\text{where $\nm\alpha<1$ and $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$}
		\tag{$\ast$}
	\]
	All isometries can be found by composing $f$ with complex conjugation (reflection in the real axis).
\end{thm}

\footnotetext{%
	If $v$ lies to the left of $\ray{zw}$, then $f(v)$ is to the left of $\ray{f(z)f(w)}$. This is the usual `right-hand rule.'%
} 


Referring to the properties in Theorem \ref{thm:mobius}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The isometries are precisely the set of Möbius transformations which map omega-points to omega-points and $D$ onto itself (Exercise \ref{exs:mobiusex}).
  \item Isometries preserve angles.
  \item The class of hyperbolic lines is preserved: any (Euclidean) circle or line intersecting the unit circle at right-angles is mapped to another such (angle-preservation is used here). 
  \item If $\Omega,\Theta$ are the omega-points on $\lin{zw}$, then (by 2 and 3), $f(\Omega)$ and $f(\Theta)$ are the omega-points for the hyperbolic line through $f(z),f(w)$. Preservation of the cross-ratio says that $f$ is an isometry:
  \[
  	d(f(z),f(w))=\nm{\ln\frac{\nm{f(z)-f(\Omega)}\nm{f(w)-f(\Theta)}}{\nm{f(z)-f(\Theta)}\nm{f(w)-f(\Omega)}}}= \nm{\ln\frac{\nm{z-\Omega}\nm{w-\Theta}}{\nm{z-\Theta}\nm{w-\Omega}}}=d(z,w)
  \]
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection*{Computation with Isometries}

How does this help us compute? The isometry ($\ast$) moves $\alpha$ to the origin, where calculating distances and angles is easy! %If desired, one can then choose $\theta$ to orient whichever direction you like along the positive $x$-axis.


\begin{example}{}{hypisomex1}
	We compute the hyperbolic distance between the points $P=\frac 12$ and $Q=\frac 23+\frac{\sqrt 2}3i$.\par
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.69\linewidth}\vspace{-4pt}
		Start by moving $P$ to the origin using an isometry with $\alpha=P=\frac 12$: the natural choice is
		\[
			f(z) =\frac{\alpha-z}{\cl\alpha z-1} 
			=\frac{1-2z}{z-2}
		\]
		Plainly $f(P)=O$. Now compute what $f$ does to $Q$:
		\[
 			f(Q) =\frac{1-\frac 43-\frac{2\sqrt 2}3i}{\frac 23-2+\frac{\sqrt 2}3i} 
 			=-\frac{1+2\sqrt 2i}{-4+\sqrt 2i} 
 			=\frac{i}{\sqrt 2}
		\]
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.3\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
		\hfill\includegraphics[alt={moving a segment so that one endpoint is at the origin}]{calc-triangle3}
	\end{minipage}
	\smallbreak
	We could have combined this with a rotation ($e^{i\theta}=e^{-\frac{i\pi}2}=-i$) to move $f(Q)$ to the positive $x$-axis, but there is no need to do so.\smallbreak
	Now let us compute distances. By Definition \ref{defn:hypdist2},
	\[
		d\bigl(f(P),f(Q)\bigr) 
		=\ln\frac{1+\nm{f(Q)}}{1-\nm{f(Q)}} 
		=\ln\frac{1+\frac 1{\sqrt 2}}{1-\frac 1{\sqrt 2}}
		=\ln\frac{\sqrt 2+1}{\sqrt 2-1}
		=\ln(3+2\sqrt 2)
	\]
	Compare this with the original cosh-formula (Definition \ref{defn:poincaredisk2}):
	\begin{gather*}
		\nm P^2=\frac 14,\qquad
		\nm Q^2=\frac 49+\frac 29=\frac 23,\qquad
		\nm{PQ}^2=\frac 1{36}+\frac 29=\frac 14\\
		\begin{aligned}
			d(P,Q)&=\cosh^{-1}\left(1+\frac{2\nm{PQ}^2}{(1-\nm P^2)(1-\nm Q^2)}\right)
			=\cosh^{-1}\left(1+\frac{\frac 24}{(1-\frac 14)(1-\frac 23)}\right)\\
			&=\cosh^{-1}3=\ln(3+\sqrt{3^2-1})=\ln(3+2\sqrt 2)\\
			&=d\big(f(P),f(Q)\big)
		\end{aligned}
	\end{gather*}
	If we trust the original cosh-formula, then the transformed points really are the same distance apart as the originals!\smallbreak
	It is worth doing some additional verifications (these are exercises):
	\begin{itemize}
	  \item The segment $\mathcolor{mRed}{\cl{PQ}}$ has equation $(x-\frac 54)^2+y^2=\frac 9{16}$. It can be checked directly that $f$ maps points on this curve to points on the $y$-axis: the segment $\mathcolor{mRed}{\cl{PQ}}$ really is transformed to $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\cl{f(P)f(Q)}}$, as claimed.
	  \item We could directly compute the co-ordinates of the omega-points for $\mathcolor{mRed}{\cl{PQ}}$ and use Definition \ref{defn:hypdist2} to verify the hyperbolic distance without resorting to the (as yet unsubstantiated) cosh-formula.
	\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\subsubsection*{Hyperbolic Trigonometry}

By employing isometries in the abstract, we develop expressions that allow us to solve hyperbolic triangles directly using only its side-lengths and angle-measures.
\medbreak

Start with a right-triangle. We may suppose an isometry has already moved the right-angle to the origin and the other sides to the positive axes at $p,iq$ as pictured. The non-hypotenuse hyperbolic side-lengths are therefore\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.65\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
	\[
		a=\ln\frac{1+p}{1-p}=\cosh^{-1}\frac{1+p^2}{1-p^2},\qquad 
		b=\cosh^{-1}\frac{1+q^2}{1-q^2}
	\]
	This uses only Definition \ref{defn:hypdist2} and the definition of inverse-cosh (footnote \ref{fn:invcosh}). To measure the hypotenuse, translate $p$ to the origin via an isometry
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.34\linewidth}\vspace{-10pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={moving a vertex to the origin to compute length and angle}]{isom-right}
\end{minipage}

\[
	f(z)=\frac{p-z}{pz-1} \implies f(iq)=\frac{p-iq}{i pq-1} \implies \nm{f(iq)}^2=\frac{p^2+q^2}{p^2q^2+1}
\]
We therefore see that
\[
	\cosh c=\frac{1+\nm{f(iq)}^2}{1-\nm{f(iq)}^2}  
	=\frac{1+p^2q^2+p^2+q^2}{1+p^2q^2-p^2-q^2} 
	=\frac{1+p^2}{1-p^2}\cdot\frac{1+q^2}{1-q^2} 
	=\cosh a\cosh b
\]
Applying the hyperbolic identity $\sinh^2b=\cosh^2b-1$, we obtain
\[
	\sinh b=\frac{2q}{1-q^2}
	\implies \tanh b=\frac{\sinh b}{\cosh b} =\frac{2q}{1+q^2}
\]
Writing $f(iq)$ in real and imaginary parts allows us to find the \textcolor{mRed}{slope} (that is, $\mathcolor{mGreen}{\tan B}$):
\[
	f(iq)=\frac{p-iq}{i pq-1} 
	=\frac{-p(1+q^2)+iq(1-p^2)}{p^2q^2+1}
	\implies \mathcolor{mGreen}{\tan B}=\frac{q(1-p^2)}{p(1+q^2)} 
	=\frac{\mathcolor{mGreen}{\tanh b}}{\mathcolor{Blue}{\sinh a}}
\]
Trigonometric identities such as $\csc^2B=1+\cot^2B$ quickly yield the other functions.

\begin{thm}{}{hyptrig}
	In a hyperbolic right-triangle with adjacent $a$, opposite $b$, and hypotenuse $c$,
	\[
		\sin B=\frac{\sinh b}{\sinh c}\qquad \cos B=\frac{\tanh a}{\tanh c}\qquad  \tan B=\frac{\tanh b}{\sinh a}\qquad \cosh c=\cosh a\cosh b
	\]
	This last is Pythagoras' Theorem for hyperbolic right-triangles.
\end{thm}

Pythagoras is easy to remember, as are the $\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}},\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}},\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$ patterns for the basic trig functions. Otherwise, these expressions (and the upcoming Corollary \ref{cor:hypcossinerule}) are open-book---they are not worth memorizing.


\pagebreak[3]


\begin{examples}{}{hypbasictrig}
	The basic rules of hyperbolic trigonometry allow us to analyze right-triangles just as in Euclidean geometry.
	
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item A right-triangle has $\mathcolor{Blue}{a}=\smash[t]{\cosh^{-1}3\approx 1.76}$ and $\mathcolor{mGreen}{b}=\smash[t]{\cosh^{-1}5\approx 2.29}$ (these are the triangles pictured in the previous analysis). We compute the remaining sides and angles.\par
	  First apply Pythagoras to compute the hypotenuse:
		\[
			\mathcolor{mRed}{\cosh c}
			=\mathcolor{Blue}{\cosh a}\mathcolor{mGreen}{\cosh b}=15
			\implies \mathcolor{mRed}{c}
			=\smash[t]{\cosh^{-1}15}\approx 3.40
		\]
		Now use the formula for sine to find the two non-right angles:
		\begin{gather*}
			\mathcolor{Blue}{\sin A}=\frac{\mathcolor{Blue}{\sinh a}}{\mathcolor{mRed}{\sinh c}} 
			=\sqrt{\frac{\cosh^2a-1}{\cosh^2 c-1}} 
			=\sqrt{\frac{3^2-1}{15^2-1}} 
			=\frac 1{2\sqrt 7} 
			\implies \mathcolor{Blue}{A}\approx \ang{10.9}\\
			\mathcolor{mGreen}{\sin B}=\frac{\mathcolor{mGreen}{\sinh b}}{\mathcolor{mRed}{\sinh c}} 
			=\sqrt{\frac{\cosh^2b-1}{\cosh^2 c-1}} 
			=\sqrt{\frac{5^2-1}{15^2-1}}
			=\sqrt{\frac 3{28}} 
			\implies \mathcolor{mGreen}{B}\approx \ang{19.1}
		\end{gather*}
		We could instead have used the other trigonometric expressions: for instance,
		\[
			\mathcolor{Blue}{\tan A}
			=\frac{\mathcolor{Blue}{\tanh a}}{\mathcolor{mGreen}{\sinh b}}
			=\frac{\sinh a}{\cosh a\sinh b}
			=\frac{\sqrt 8}{3\sqrt{24}} 
			=\frac 1{3\sqrt 3} \implies A=\tan^{-1}\frac 1{3\sqrt 3}\approx \mathcolor{Blue}{A}\approx \ang{10.9}
		\]
	
	  \item\label{ex:hyperrogueparallel} Consider the pictured Lambert quadrilateral with side-lengths $a,b,v,h$ and diagonal $d$. Applying the sine, cosine and tangent formulæ,\par
	  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.61\linewidth}\vspace{-13pt}
	  	\begin{gather*}
				\sin\beta=\frac{\sinh b}{\sinh d}\qquad
				\cos\beta=\sin\left(\frac\pi 2-\beta\right)=\frac{\sinh h}{\sinh d}\\
				\implies \frac{\tanh b}{\sinh a}=\tan\beta=\frac{\sin\beta}{\cos\beta} =\frac{\sinh b}{\sinh h}\\
				\implies \sinh h=\sinh a\cosh b
			\end{gather*}
	  \end{minipage}
	  \hfill
	  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.35\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
	  	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={relating length data for a quadrilateral}]{isom-lambert}

		By doubling the quadrilateral, we obtain a Saccheri quadrilateral with base $2a$, congruent sides $b$, and summit $2h$. Since $\cosh b>1$ and is strictly increasing, observe:
		\begin{itemize}
		  \item The summit of a Saccheri quadrilateral is \emph{longer} than its base.
		  \item If the base $2a$ is fixed, the summit is a \emph{strictly increasing} function of the side-length $b$.
		\end{itemize}
		If such a line exists, a common perpendicular describes the shortest path between two parallel lines.

The goal of trigonometry is to `solve' triangles: given minimal numerical data, to compute the remaining sides and angles. As in Euclidean geometry, we can drop perpendiculars and using the results of Theorem \ref{thm:hyptrig}. Alternatively, we can generalize this procedure by developing the sine and cosine rules.


\pagebreak[3]


\begin{cor}{Sine/Cosine rules and the Cosh-distance formula}{hypcossinerule}
	Label a general triangle with angle-measures $A,B,C$ opposite sides with (hyperbolic) lengths $a,b,c$.
	\begin{description}
		\item[Sine Rule] This is proved exactly as the easy method in Euclidean geometry. \par
		\begin{minipage}[t]{0.74\linewidth}\vspace{-8pt}
		 	Drop a perpendicular from $C$ to obtain $\sin A=\frac{\sinh h}{\sinh b}$ and $\sin B=\frac{\sinh h}{\sinh a}$. Eliminate $\sinh h$ for the first equality in
			\[
				\frac{\sinh a}{\sin A}=\frac{\sinh b}{\sin B}=\frac{\sinh c}{\sin C}
			\]
			Drop a different altitude for the remaining equality.
		\end{minipage}
		\hfill
		\begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\linewidth}\vspace{-15pt}
			\hfill\includegraphics[alt={proving the hyperbolic sine rule}]{isom-sine}
		\end{minipage}
			
		\item[Cosine Rule I] Repeat the argument of Theorem \ref{thm:hyptrig} for a triangle with vertices $0,p$ and $qe^{iC}$ to obtain
		\[
			\cosh c=\cosh a\cosh b-\sinh a\sinh b\cos C
		\]
		Expressing the right side in terms of $p,q$ ($\cosh a=\frac{1+p^2}{1-p^2}$, \ etc.) yields the original cosh-formula for distance (Definition \ref{defn:poincaredisk2}).
		\item[Cosine Rule II] Hyperbolic geometry admits a second version (for a proof see Exercise \ref{exs:hcosruleII}),
		\[
			\cos C=\sin A\sin B\cosh c-\cos A\cos B
		\]\vspace{-4pt}
	\end{description}
\end{cor}


In hyperbolic geometry, the triangle congruence theorems (SAS, ASA, SSS, SAA \emph{and} AAA) provide suitable minimal data. The second version of the cosine rule has no analogue in Euclidean geometry---it is particularly helpful for solving triangles given ASA or AAA data.\footnote{%
	Unlike in Euclidean geometry, knowing two angles doesn't automatically give you the third! For SAS and SSS start with the cosine rule. SAA data is best solved by dropping a perpendicular and using Theorem \ref{thm:hyptrig}.%
}


\begin{examples}{}{hyptrig}
	We give four examples of solving hyperbolic triangles.
	
	\begin{enumerate}
	  \item (SAS)\lstsp An isosceles triangle has $C=\frac{\pi}3$ and $a=b=\cosh^{-1}2\approx 1.32$. We have\par
	  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.69\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
	  	\[
	  		\mathcolor{Blue}{\sinh a}=\mathcolor{mGreen}{\sinh b} = \mathcolor{Blue}{\smash[t]{\sqrt{\cosh^2a-1}}}=\sqrt 3
	  	\]
	  	By the sine and cosine rules,
			\begin{gather*}
				\mathcolor{mRed}{\cosh c}
					=\mathcolor{Blue}{2} \cdot \mathcolor{mGreen}{2} 
					-\mathcolor{Blue}{\sqrt 3} \mathcolor{mGreen}{\sqrt 3}
					\cdot \mathcolor{mGreen}{\frac 12} 
					=\frac 52\implies \mathcolor{mRed}{c}=\cosh^{-1}\frac 52\approx 1.57\\
				\mathcolor{mGreen}{\sin B}
					=\mathcolor{Blue}{\sin A}
					=\frac{\mathcolor{mRed}{\sin C}\mathcolor{Blue}{\sinh a}}{\mathcolor{mRed}{\sinh c}} 
					=\mathcolor{mRed}{\frac{\sqrt 3}2}
					\cdot\frac{\mathcolor{Blue}{\sqrt 3}}{\mathcolor{mRed}{\sqrt{21/4}}} 
					=\frac 3{\sqrt{21}}=\sqrt{\frac 37}\\
				\implies \mathcolor{mGreen}{A}=\mathcolor{Blue}{B}
				\approx \ang{40.9}
			\end{gather*}
	  \end{minipage}
	  \hfill
	  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.3\linewidth}\vspace{-10pt}
	  	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={solving a hyperbolic triangle given S A S data}]{isom-trigsas}
	  \end{minipage}

		\pagebreak[3]
	 		 	
	 	\item (Equilateral AAA)\lstsp An equilateral triangle has interior angles $\ang{30}$. Applying the second cosine rule, we see that\par
	 	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.65\linewidth}\vspace{-12pt}
	 		\begin{gather*}
	 			\cosh c 
	 			=\frac{\mathcolor{Blue}{\cos A}\mathcolor{mGreen}{\cos B}
	 				+\mathcolor{mRed}{\cos C}}{\mathcolor{Blue}{\sin A}\mathcolor{mGreen}{\sin B}} 
	 			=\frac{\frac{\sqrt 3}2\cdot\frac{\sqrt 3}2
	 				+\frac{\sqrt 3}2}{\frac 12\cdot\frac 12}

\subsubsection*{Hyperbolic Tilings (just for fun!)}\label{pg:hyptiling}

Example \ref*{ex:hyptrig}.\ref{ex:rtangleaaa} can be used to construct regular tilings of hyperbolic space. Take eight congruent copies of the triangle and arrange them around the origin as in the first picture. Now reflect the quadrilateral over each of its edges and repeat the process in all directions ad infinitum. The result is a regular tiling of hyperbolic space comprising \emph{four-sided} figures with \emph{six} meeting at every vertex!

\begin{center}
	\begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\qquad}c@{\qquad}c@{}}
		\includegraphics[height=140pt,alt={a hyperbolic tiling where six regular quadrilaterals meet at each vertex}]{isom-tiling}
		&\includegraphics[alt={the fundamental triangle for a regular hyperbolic tiling}]{isom-right3}
		&\includegraphics[height=140pt,alt={a hyperbolic tiling where four regular pentagons meet at each vertex}]{tiling}\\[5pt]
		$(m,n)=(4,6)$
		&The fundamental triangle
		&$(m,n)=(5,4)$
	\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In hyperbolic space, many different regular tilings are possible. Suppose such is to be made using regular $m$-sided polygons, $n$ of which are to meet at each vertex: each polygon comprises $2m$ copies of the pictured \emph{fundamental right-triangle}, whose angles must be $\frac\pi 2, \frac\pi m$ and $\frac\pi n$. Since the angles sum to less than $\pi$ radians, we see that there exists a regular tiling of hyperbolic space precisely when
	\[
		\frac\pi 2+\frac\pi m+\frac\pi n<\pi\iff (m-2)(n-2)>4
	\]
	The first example is $m=4$ and $n=6$. In the other picture tiling, $n=4$ pentagons ($m=5$) meet at each vertex (the interior of each pentagon has been colored congruently for fun). This pentagonal tiling was produced using the tools found
	\href{http://www.malinc.se/noneuclidean/en/poincaretiling.php}{here} and \href{http://www.malinc.se/m/ImageTiling.php}{here}: have a play!\medbreak
	
	The multitude of possible tilings in hyperbolic geometry is in contrast to Euclidean geometry, where a regular tiling requires \emph{equality}
	\[
		(m-2)(n-2)=4
	\]
	The three solutions $(m,n)=(3,6)$, $(4,4)$, $(6,3)$ correspond to the only tilings of Euclidean geometry by regular polygons (equilateral triangles, squares and regular hexagons); unlike in hyperbolic geometry, Euclidean tilings may have arbitrary side-lengths.\medbreak
	
	For related fun, look up M.C.\,Escher's \emph{Circle Limit} artworks, some of which are based on hyperbolic tilings.
	If you want an excuse to play video games while pretending to study geometry, have a look at \href{http://www.roguetemple.com/z/hyper/}{Hyper Rogue}, which relies on (sometimes irregular) tilings of hyperbolic space.

\subsubsection*{The Poincaré Disk for Differential Geometers (non-examinable)}\label{pg:hyp1stform}

Most of this last optional section should be accessible to anyone who's taken basic vector-calculus. All we really need is the Poincaré disk model with its distance function $d(z,w)$ and a description of the isometries (Theorems \ref{thm:mobius}, \ref{thm:hypisomclass}).\smallbreak

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.69\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	Consider the infinitesimally separated points $z$ and $z+\dz$. Map $z$ to the origin via an isometry
	\[
		f:\xi\mapsto \frac{z-\xi}{\cl z\xi-1}
	\]
	Then $z+\dz$ is mapped to
	\[
		P:=f(z+\dz)=\frac{-\dz}{\cl z(z+\dz)-1} =\frac{\dz}{1-\nm z^2}
	\]
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.3\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	\hfill\includegraphics[alt={using an isometry to move calculations to the origin}]{isom-arclength}
\end{minipage}
\medbreak
where we deleted $\cl z\,\dz$ since it is infinitesimal compared to $1-\nm z^2$.\smallbreak
Since isometries preserve length and angle, this construction has several consequences.

\subsubsection*{Infinitesimal distance, arc-length, and geodesics}

The hyperbolic distance from $z$ to $z+\dz$ is
\[
	d(z,z+\dz)=d(O,P)=\ln\frac{1+\nm P}{1-\nm P} =\ln(1+\nm{P})-\ln(1-\nm{P})=2\nm{P}=\frac{2\nm{\dz}}{1-\nm{z(t)}^2} \tag{$\ast$}
\]
where the approximation $\ln(1\pm\nm P)=\pm\nm P$ is used since $\nm P$ is infinitesimal. If $z(t)$ parametrizes a curve in the disk, then the infinitesimal distance formula allows us to compute its arc-length
\[
	\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{2\nm{z'(t)}}{1-\nm{z(t)}^2}\,\dt
\]

	
\begin{example}{Circles and `hyperbolic $\pi$'}{hypcircleparam}
	Suppose that a circle has hyperbolic radius $\delta$. By moving its center to the origin via an isometry, we may parametrize in the usual manner:
	\[
		z(t)=r\twovec{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta},\quad \theta\in[0,2\pi)
		\quad\text{where}\quad 
		\delta=\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}
		\quad\text{equivalently}\quad
		r=\frac{e^\delta-1}{e^\delta+1}
	\]
	Its circumference (hyperbolic arc-length) is then
	\[
		\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{2 r}{1-r^2}\,\dth =\frac{4\pi r}{1-r^2} =2\pi\sinh\delta =2\pi\left(\delta+\frac{1}{3!}\delta^3+\frac{1}{4!}\delta^5+\cdots\right)>2\pi\delta
	\]
	where we used the Maclaurin series to compare.\smallbreak
	A hyperbolic circle has a \emph{larger} circumference\,:\,diameter ratio than for a Euclidean circle ($\pi$). Moreover, this ratio is \emph{not constant}: one might say that the hyperbolic version of $\pi$ is a function ($\frac{\pi\sinh\delta}\delta$)!
\end{example}


	
Our arc-length integral approach also allows us to show that hyperbolic lines are really what we want them to be: lines of shortest distance between points.
	
\begin{thm}{}{}
	The geodesics---paths of minimal length between two points---in the Poincaré disk are precisely the hyperbolic lines.
\end{thm}

Following the comments on page \pageref{sec:hyperiso}, the distance function really does define the concept of hyperbolic line.

\begin{proof}
	First suppose $b$ lies on the positive $x$-axis. Parametrize a curve from $0$ to $b$ via
	\[
		z(t)=x(t)+iy(t)\quad\text{where}\quad 0\le t\le 1,\quad z(0)=0,\quad z(1)=b
	\]
	Its arc-length satisfies
	\begin{align*}
		\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2\nm{z'(t)}}{1-\nm{z(t)}^2}\,\dt
		&=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}}{1-x^2-y^2}\,\dt\ge \int_{0}^{1}\frac{2\nm{x'}}{1-x^2}\,\dt\ge \int_{0}^{1}\frac{2x'(t)}{1-x(t)^2}\,\dt =\int_0^b\frac{2\,\dx}{1-x^2}\\
		&=\ln\frac{1+b}{1-b}=d(0,b)
	\end{align*}
	where we have equality if and only if $y(t)\equiv 0$ and $x(t)$ is increasing. The length-minimizing path is therefore along the $x$-axis.\smallbreak
	More generally, given points $A,B$, apply an isometry $f$ such that $f(A)=0$ and $f(B)=b$ lies on the positive $x$-axis. The geodesic from $A$ to $B$ is therefore the image of the segment $\cl{0b}$ under the inverse isometry $f^{-1}$. By the properties of Möbius transforms, this is an arc of a Euclidean circle through $A,B$ intersecting the unit circle at right-angles, our original definition of a hyperbolic line.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Area Computation}

If $\dx$ and $i\dy$ are infinitesimal horizontal and vertical changes in $z=x+iy$, then the area of the infinitesimal rectangle spanned by $z\to z+\dx$ and $z\to z+i\dy$ is the area element
\[
	\dA=\frac{2\,\dx}{1-\nm z^2}\frac{2\,\dy}{1-\nm z^2} =\frac{4\,\dx\,\dy}{(1-x^2-y^2)^2}
\]
The area of a region $R$ in the Poincaré disk is therefore given by the double integral
\[
	\iint_R\frac{4\,\dx\dy}{(1-x^2-y^2)^2}=\iint_R\frac{4r\,\dr\,\dth}{(1-r^2)^2} =\iint_R\sinh\delta\,\D\delta\,\dth
\]
where the last expression is written in polar co-ordinates using the hyperbolic distance $\delta$. In this way the measure of area also depends on the distance function.
	
\begin{example*}{\ref{ex:hypcircleparam}, cont}
	The area of a hyperbolic circle with hyperbolic radius $\delta$ is
	\[
		\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\delta\sinh\delta\,\D\delta\,\dth =2\pi(\cosh\delta-1) =\pi\left(\delta^2+\frac{2}{4!}\delta^4+\frac{2}{6!}\delta^6+\cdots\right)>\pi\delta^2
	\]
	Again, this is larger than you'd expect in Euclidean geometry.
\end{example*}



\subsubsection*{Angle Measure and the First Fundamental Form}\label{pg:hypareaext}

If we repeat the distance translation ($\ast$, page \pageref{pg:hyp1stform}) for a second infinitesimal \textcolor{mGreen}{segment} $z\to z+\dw$, it can be checked that the \textcolor{Brown}{angle} between the original segments is precisely that between the infinitesimal vectors $\dz$ and $\dw$. This is precisely the conformality observation in Theorem \ref{thm:mobius} and moreover shows how the distance function determines the angle measure.\smallbreak

If you've studied differential geometry, then a more formal way to think about this is to use the \emph{first fundamental form} or \emph{metric}: essentially the dot product of infinitesimal tangent vectors. For the Poincaré disk model, ($\ast$) says that this is
\[
	\I =\frac{4(\dx^2+\dy^2)}{(1-x^2-y^2)^2} =\frac{4(\dr^2+r^2\,\dth^2)}{(1-r^2)^2} 
\]
Since this is a scalar multiple of the standard Euclidean metric $\dx^2+\dy^2$, angle measures are identical.\footnote{%
	Recall that the angle $\psi$ between vectors $\vu,\vv$ satisfies $\vu\cdot\vv=\nm{\vu}\nm{\vv}\cos\psi$. For infinitesimal vectors we use $\I=\lambda^2(\dx^2+\dy^2)$ instead of the dot product, where $\lambda=\frac 2{1-r^2}$. The resulting angle is the same as if we use the Euclidean metric $\dx^2+\dy^2$, since factors of $\lambda^2$ cancel on both sides.
}
It also gels with the fact that arc-length is the integral
\[
	\int\sqrt{\I\bigl(z'(t),z'(t)\bigr)}\,\dt
\]
Using this language, two of the major theorems of introductory differential geometry quickly put a couple of remaining issues to bed.
\begin{description}
	\item[Gauss' Theorem Egregium] The first fundamental form determines the \emph{Gaussian curvature} $K$. In this case $K=-1$ is constant and negative, as you should easily be able to verify if you've studied differential geometry.
	\item[Gauss--Bonnet Theorem] The angle-sum $\Sigma_{\triangle}$ of a geodesic triangle in a space with Gaussian curvature $K$ satisfies
	\[
		\Sigma_{\triangle}-\pi=\iint_{\triangle} K
	\]
	This establishes our earlier assertion that $\operatorname{Area}\triangle =\pi-\Sigma_{\triangle}$ (Corollary \ref{cor:hypareaangle}).
\end{description}
